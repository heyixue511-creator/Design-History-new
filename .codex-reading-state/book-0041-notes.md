## Introduction

- Opens with Manchester Chamber of Commerce in 1860, when Britain held roughly two-thirds of world cotton spindles and connected slave plantations, wage-labor factories, merchants and world markets.
- Book explains rise and fall of a Europe-dominated cotton empire as a history of global capitalism, not a national industrialization story.
- Central distinction:
  - **war capitalism** (from sixteenth century): countryside, imperial expansion, armed trade, land expropriation, slavery / bodily coercion, private sovereignty and weak security for non-European property;
  - **industrial capitalism** (from late eighteenth century): factories, wage labor, stronger administrative / military / judicial / infrastructural states, property and contract institutions, while remaining linked to slavery and expropriated land.
- Rejects essentialist explanations of European divergence based on religion, Enlightenment, climate, geography or benign liberal institutions. Britain was imperial, protectionist, highly taxed, war-making, indebted and undemocratic.
- Cotton matters more than sugar, tobacco, indigo or rice for this argument because it joined two labor-intensive stages—fields and factories—and generated plantation slavery, a large industrial proletariat, global markets and manufacturing profits.
- Tracks cotton from fields to transport, finance, factories, sale and consumption; argues that apparently opposed systems—slavery/free labor, states/markets, colonialism/free trade, industrialization/deindustrialization—formed one changing global complex.
- Global commodity biography is the method. National frames conceal how decisions in Liverpool, Mississippi, Mulhouse, Manchester, India and the Ottoman countryside affected one another.
- Women’s labor was foundational despite industrial capitalism often being remembered as male. Countryside, coercion and raw-material production must remain central, not be eclipsed by cities, coal, rail and steel.
- The present-day claims and statistics are dated to 2013–2014 and must not be treated as current.

## Chapter 1 — The Rise of a Global Commodity

- Begins with cotton cultivation, spinning, backstrap weaving, dyeing and tribute in villages within the Aztec imperial system; cotton was grown alongside subsistence crops and processed through intensive skilled household labor.
- Cotton manufacture developed independently in South Asia, the Americas and Africa. Four domesticated species discussed: `G. hirsutum`, `G. barbadense`, `G. herbaceum`, `G. arboreum`; today’s dominance of American upland is a later historical outcome.
- Archaeological and textual evidence: Indus / Mehrgarh / Mohenjo-Daro, coastal Peru, Mesoamerica, Nubia / Meroë, later West Africa; many very early dates and “first” formulations require archaeology cross-check.
- South Asia led global production for millennia. Indian skills included roller ginning, bow cleaning, spindle / wheel spinning, sophisticated looms, dyeing and extremely fine muslins, chintzes and calicoes.
- Cotton was central in pre-Columbian Mexico and Peru, with tribute, status, exchange, religious, medicinal, armor and funerary uses. Claimed output comparisons (e.g. pre-Columbian Mexico vs. U.S. 1816) need independent verification.
- Islam accelerated cotton cultivation, spinning, weaving, modest dress demand and trade across West Asia, North / West Africa, Iberia and the Mediterranean. The author sometimes compresses heterogeneous religious and regional processes.
- China adopted cotton from border regions; under Yuan / Ming it displaced some ramie, became tax payment and military / official clothing, and supported north–lower Yangtze division of labor. Japan expanded cotton after c.1525–1550.
- Before eighteenth-century monoculture, households intercropped cotton with food. Cotton growing was a risk-diversification strategy; raw cotton usually stayed near its growing zone, while cloth—with high value-to-weight and durability—traveled widely and functioned as tribute, tax, currency and store of value.
- Gender division: women almost everywhere dominated spinning; weaving varied. Household responsibilities and intermittent spinning were mutually reinforcing. This should not be naturalized: the author explains historical organization, and later factory gender divisions reproduced it.
- Household self-provision remained dominant, but urban workshops and merchant putting-out networks grew in India, China, the Ottoman world, West Africa and Japan. Merchant control varied; Indian weavers often owned tools and retained some disposal power, while Ottoman and Chinese merchants could control inputs and output more tightly.
- Technological change before mechanization included Asian roller gins, bows, spinning wheel, treadle loom, irrigation, seed selection and domestication. Expansion still depended mainly on more people spending more time, not dramatic productivity growth.
- India’s textiles became the first major global consumer commodity, moving through the Indian Ocean, Red Sea, Persian Gulf, overland Middle East, Africa, Southeast Asia, China and eventually Europe. Fabric names and trade terms traveled across languages.
- Europe was peripheral. Islamic Iberia and Sicily introduced manufacture; northern Italy from twelfth century and southern Germany from fourteenth century built fustian industries using imported Levantine cotton, Asian / Islamic technologies, guild and putting-out labor, merchant capital and long-distance trade.
- Northern Italian / German industries remained vulnerable because they lacked control over raw cotton. Ottoman consolidation, export restrictions, shifts from Mediterranean to Atlantic routes and war contributed to decline. The chapter ends by foreshadowing that later Europeans would seek to subjugate suppliers rather than merely buy from them.
- Visuals: source contains a global cotton map and other map-like material; project rule excludes maps / flags / emblems from textbook use.

## Chapter 2 — Building War Capitalism

- Columbus (1492) and da Gama (1497–98) are used as turning points in European projection of military / commercial power into the Americas and Indian Ocean. The argument is not that global cotton trade began then, but that Europeans reorganized it through armed shipping, territorial conquest and chartered companies.
- Portuguese, Dutch, English, Danish and French East India companies entered old Indian Ocean networks. European “factories” were fortified commercial warehouses / agencies, not mechanized plants.
- At first European merchants in India remained dependent on banias, inland merchants, bankers and weavers. They specified quality, design, price and delivery, while local intermediaries advanced funds. Weavers generally owned tools, controlled rhythm and could sell to competitors; European demand could raise prices.
- Naval firepower and faster shipping gradually marginalized Indian, Arab and Ottoman long-distance traders. This was not primarily a story of superior European goods or textile technology.
- American conquest, seizure of precious metals, plantation expansion and forced deportation of Africans linked Indian textiles, African slave trade and American commodities. Cotton cloth often made up more than half the exchange value in documented slave purchases; African traders / consumers exercised specific and changing tastes rather than accepting generic “trinkets.”
- **War capitalism** consists of imperial expansion, expropriation, slavery, armed trade, chartered companies and privatized sovereignty. Europe divided an ordered domestic “inside” from a coercive colonial “outside,” where property seizure and bodily domination made markets.
- European Atlantic cotton manufacture arose in Flemish, French and especially Lancashire countryside. Putting-out merchants advanced raw cotton / money to rural families. English spinners and weavers often lacked independent material / market access and were more subordinated than many Indian counterparts.
- Before 1770 Europe sourced small amounts of cotton through diversified Ottoman, Indian, African and West Indian trade. With the West Indies, slave labor indicated the later plantation model; merchants generally had little control over hinterland production elsewhere.
- After East India Company territorial rule in Bengal (1765), British traders replaced or subordinated banias with payroll agents, advances, inspection, taxation, legal attachment, soldiers and corporal punishment. Weavers resisted by moving, clandestine sale, collective complaint and using competition. Company control was real but incomplete.
- Author cites a sharp fall in producer share and “unprecedented mortality” among weavers; all numbers and causal scope need local economic-history verification.
- European states combined growing Indian cloth exports with protection at home: tariffs, import / wearing bans, criminal penalties and in France even forced labor / severe sanctions. Indian textiles for African slave trade were exempted. Protectionism created room for domestic cotton manufacture rather than simply defending wool / linen.
- Indian technology and design were appropriated through observation reports, travel, samples, copying and state-backed “industrial espionage.” Europe’s calico printing and quality standards depended on Asian knowledge.
- Overseas markets—especially Africa and the Americas—absorbed growing European output. African demand compelled British makers to imitate Indian textiles and sell under Indian names. Markets captured through imperial / slave networks created incentives for mechanization.
- Profits and institutions of war capitalism fed manufacturing credit, insurance, finance, shipping, public credit and national defense. The chapter argues state power and global networks preceded the factory revolution.
- Central caution: the chapter sometimes opposes older “mutually advantageous” Afro-Asian-American networks to European hierarchical networks too cleanly. Pre-European coercion, tribute, caste / status and state power were already present; difference is scale / form and requires regional cross-check.

## Chapter 3 — The Wages of War Capitalism

- Samuel Greg’s Quarry Bank Mill (1784) anchors the move from putting-out to water-powered spinning with orphan / parish-apprentice children, adult wage workers, Caribbean cotton and export markets. Greg’s family capital, Dominica plantation, slave-trade and merchant ties directly join factory industrialization to war capitalism.
- Author argues British invention cannot be explained only by inventors, integrated markets, geography, dissenting religion or entrepreneurial institutions. British control of global cotton networks, imperial markets, Asian knowledge, slave-produced raw material and a war-making state supplied crucial conditions.
- High British wages relative to India created incentive to raise labor productivity. Protection alone could shelter domestic markets, but global competition required cheaper production and scale.
- Technical sequence:
  - Kay flying shuttle (1733) raised weaving output and intensified spinning bottleneck;
  - Hargreaves spinning jenny (1760s) multiplied hand-powered spindles;
  - Arkwright water frame (1769) enabled continuous water-powered spinning and factory concentration;
  - Crompton mule (1779) combined jenny / frame principles for finer, stronger yarn;
  - Cartwright power loom (1785) developed more slowly;
  - later steam and automated mules deepened productivity.
- Macro-inventions were practical workshop solutions by artisans with limited formal education, often resisted by workers and imperfectly rewarded by patents. Arkwright profited; Kay / Hargreaves did not. Britain criminalized machinery export.
- New machines did more than substitute labor: central power and large machinery moved work into mills, synchronized pace and concentrated surveillance. Women and children performed cleaning, carding, drawing and spinning tasks; fire, dust, noise and long hours were material conditions.
- Productivity and price claims are dramatic (e.g. 370-fold spinning productivity and sharp yarn / muslin price declines); use as P1 synthesis only until checked against specialist quantitative history.
- India / China possessed older machine knowledge, commercial cotton systems and technical capability. Beckert explains non-mechanization through lack of equivalent global-node control, greater peasant land security, household gender organization / low opportunity cost, intermediaries and stronger resistance capacity. These broad comparisons risk flattening regional variation and naturalizing women’s “cheap” labor.
- British factories multiplied rapidly, often beginning with modest fixed capital and expanding through retained profits plus Liverpool / London merchant credit. Personal networks and growing circulating capital gradually raised barriers to entry.
- Cotton became Britain’s leading value-added and employment sector and Lancashire a concentrated production region. Exports—not the small domestic market—drove the boom; Africa, plantations / settler Americas and then wider markets absorbed British cloth.
- British yarn / cloth displaced Indian exports and contributed to Dhaka / Bengal deindustrialization, hunger, migration into agriculture and producer impoverishment. Causal magnitude and chronology need debate with Indian economic history; colonial company coercion and mechanized price competition are both in the author’s account.
- Industrial capitalism required a powerful state able to protect markets, enforce contracts / property, tax, build infrastructure, mobilize wage labor and project empire. Its “free market” was built by long lists of tariffs, prohibitions and regulation.
- Manufacturers converted economic weight into politics, especially after the 1832 Reform Act. Capital became territorialized / nationalized through dependence on a specific state; this later gave organized workers leverage through that state.
- Britain externalized land seizure, slavery and raw-material coercion while using wage labor and impersonal law domestically. Industrial capitalism did not replace war capitalism: from c.1770–1860 its cotton demand intensified it abroad.
- Chapter closing contrast: factory productivity and state capacity generated “civilization” and extreme urban pollution / brutalization at once; Tocqueville’s Manchester language is a historical observer’s judgment, not neutral description.

## Chapter 4 — Capturing Labor, Conquering Land

- Mechanized British cotton manufacture depended on raw material that could not be grown locally. In the 1780s British supplies came from the Ottoman Empire, the Caribbean, India, Africa and Brazil; the United States had not yet become a meaningful exporter.
- Rapid growth in spinning enlarged raw-cotton demand and raised prices. The chapter treats the resulting “supply problem” as a struggle to reorganize distant land and labor, not as a neutral response by already integrated markets.
- Ottoman Anatolia and Macedonia did not readily divert land and labor from subsistence, local textile production and established agrarian relations. Local rulers, cultivators and artisans retained political and economic capacities that limited European command. The author’s language of a region that failed to “revolutionize” production must not be reproduced as a developmental deficiency.
- The East India Company was ambivalent about expanding Indian raw-cotton exports: officials feared damage to textile manufacture and land revenue, diversion from food crops and famine. Infrastructure, fiber length, cleaning and labor organization also constrained supply. These tensions show that colonial authorities did not pursue a single coherent cotton policy.
- The Caribbean and northeastern Brazil initially offered greater supply elasticity because plantation slavery, expropriated land, merchant capital, ports and imperial administration were already in place. Cotton often occupied new land or served as a transitional plantation crop before sugar or coffee.
- Barbados, Tobago, the Bahamas, Jamaica, Grenada, Dominica, Saint-Domingue, Guyana, Pernambuco and Maranhão formed shifting cotton frontiers. Production repeatedly moved when soils declined, other plantation crops became more profitable or political upheaval interrupted coercive labor regimes.
- Saint-Domingue’s late-eighteenth-century cotton expansion relied on a large increase in the forced importation of enslaved Africans. Brazil’s first export surge likewise came principally from larger slave plantations, despite cotton’s later association with small producers.
- Imperial states actively promoted cotton through prizes, tariff preferences, seed collection, land grants, governors and diplomatic / commercial pressure. This evidence challenges accounts in which states merely secured an external background for private enterprise.
- Beckert uses the concept of **second slavery** to connect the late expansion of Atlantic slavery to industrial capitalism. The claim that nearly half of all people carried across the Atlantic in the slave trade arrived after 1780, and the many regional output figures, require verification against the current slave-trade literature.
- The core ecological argument is counterfactual: distant plantations allowed Britain to externalize the enormous land and agricultural-labor requirements of industrialization. Estimates of hypothetical British cotton acreage, workers and substitute sheep pasture are analytically useful but should be presented as the author’s calculations, not observed facts.
- Supply based on conquest and coerced labor was “elastic” precisely because land could be seized and enslaved people could be deported or moved. The chapter therefore recasts apparent factor availability as the political product of war capitalism.
- The Haitian Revolution (1791 onward) was a decisive reversal initiated by enslaved people. It abolished slavery in Saint-Domingue, created Haiti and sharply reduced a major source of European cotton. The disruption raised prices and exposed the instability of a supply system built on plantation coercion.
- Revolutionary refugees carried plantation expertise to Cuba and the United States, while the loss of Saint-Domingue increased the incentive for U.S. planters to grow cotton. The chapter ends by shifting from a dispersed Atlantic supply system to the United States’ unusually concentrated combination of land seizure and slavery.
- The source includes a slave-ship illustration and several geographic / quantitative graphics. Any possible visual use requires separate ethical and source review; maps are excluded by the textbook rules.

## Chapter 5 — Slavery Takes Command

- The chapter explains the United States’ rapid emergence after the 1780s as Europe’s principal cotton supplier through an interlocking system of plantation slavery, Indigenous land expropriation, state expansion, Atlantic finance, transport infrastructure, plant knowledge and mechanical ginning.
- U.S. commercial cotton began from a very small base. Sea Island cotton expanded along the Georgia / South Carolina coast using seeds and knowledge circulating from the Bahamas, Mediterranean and Caribbean; upland cotton made interior expansion possible.
- Eli Whitney’s 1793 gin removed a processing bottleneck for short-staple upland cotton. The author treats the machine as necessary but insufficient: it became transformative only within an expanding regime of seized land and enslaved labor. Claims such as a fiftyfold productivity gain and immediate land-price effects require specialist verification.
- The Haitian Revolution both removed Saint-Domingue as a leading supplier and dispersed planter expertise, raising prices and encouraging U.S. expansion. It also remained a warning to planters and manufacturers that enslaved people could destroy the regime imposed on them.
- Cotton and slavery expanded together. Enslaved people were forcibly transported from the upper to the lower South through both renewed overseas importation before 1808 and a vast internal slave trade; family separation, sale, confinement, whipping and terror were constitutive economic mechanisms, not incidental abuses.
- Cotton cultivation exhausted soils, prompting a moving frontier from South Carolina and Georgia through Alabama, Louisiana and Mississippi to Arkansas and Texas. Production figures by state reveal this movement, but source maps are excluded from textbook use.
- The state created “available” land through the Louisiana Purchase, acquisition of Florida, annexation of Texas, treaties imposed under duress, war and forced removal of Creek, Chickasaw, Choctaw, Cherokee and Seminole peoples. The term “empty land” records a settler legal fiction produced by epidemic, violence and dispossession.
- Rivers, steamboats, roads and railways tied plantation hinterlands to New Orleans and Atlantic ports. Modern transport technology therefore enabled, rather than displaced, intensified forced labor.
- Planter political dominance was secured in federal institutions, the Constitution’s three-fifths clause, slaveholding presidencies / courts and southern state governments. This power aligned territorial expansion, removal policy, infrastructure and protection of human property with cotton interests.
- The Yazoo–Mississippi Delta case joins Choctaw dispossession, highly capitalized plantations, a very large enslaved majority, navigation and European finance. Its output statistics and property valuations need independent checking.
- Transatlantic credit often used enslaved people as mortgage collateral. This made human property a mechanism for allocating European capital to new plantations and linked banks, merchants, factors and mills directly to slavery.
- Planters appropriated Indigenous and transregional seed knowledge, including Mexican germplasm, and developed many locally adapted cultivars. Agricultural journals, associations and account books institutionalized knowledge of seed selection, field organization, markets and coercive labor management.
- Gang labor, quotas, bookkeeping and the systematic mobilization of women and children intensified output. Beckert draws on Edward Baptist and management history to argue that torture and plantation accounting drove productivity; this influential but debated quantitative claim must be attributed and cross-checked.
- Narratives by formerly enslaved John Brown and Henry Bibb make bodily violence legible from workers’ testimony. They should not be subordinated to planter accounting or reproduced as spectacle; if used, frame them as evidence of enslaved people’s knowledge and resistance.
- Large plantations produced most cotton, although smaller nonslaveholding / slaveholding farmers also participated. Cotton’s profitability raised slave prices, financed mansions and further purchases, and gave southern planters extraordinary political confidence.
- British and continental mills grew dependent on U.S. slavery even as abolitionists challenged it. Manufacturers feared American domestic industrialization, continental competition, political sectional conflict, emancipation and slave rebellion.
- Efforts to create an alternative source in India show the limits of colonial command before the 1860s. Indian cultivators prioritized food, household and local textile markets, retained land and labor autonomy, used locally adapted seeds / gins and resisted wage farms, alien experimental stations and loss of common “waste” land.
- Colonial officials pathologized rational decisions as laziness, prejudice or lack of enterprise. The historical vocabulary must be quoted critically, with cultivator knowledge, subsistence risk and resistance foregrounded.
- British initiatives included seeds, American gins / planters, experimental farms, surveys, fraud laws, territorial acquisition, infrastructure proposals and demands for direct merchant access to cultivators. Most failed because U.S. plantation practices did not fit Indian rainfall, capital, labor, land-use and market relations.
- Indian merchants and brokers continued to dominate internal exchange. European merchants lacked inland institutions and were unwilling to risk the capital required for advances, storage and processing; the East India Company and private merchants blamed one another.
- Egypt offered a different coercive alternative under Muhammad Ali: state-ordered cotton cultivation, corvée labor, compulsory sale, controlled prices, canals, roads and state monopoly. This was not “free labor”; coercion was exerted primarily through the state rather than private slaveholders.
- Older household, local and regional cotton circuits persisted across India, China, Japan, Africa and Latin America, perhaps consuming more cotton than mills at mid-century. Cheap slave-grown industrial cloth progressively undermined them, producing what the chapter calls the “making of a periphery.”
- The chapter’s causal conclusion is relational: U.S. dominance was not a natural gift of soil and climate. It resulted from unusually complete command of land, labor, capital and government, while areas whose cultivators and local elites retained greater control were less easily remade for export.

## Chapter 6 — Industrial Capitalism Takes Wing

- Mechanized cotton manufacture spread rapidly but unevenly from Britain to continental Europe, the United States, Russia, Egypt, Mexico and eventually farther afield. Valladolid in Yucatán and the Wiesental / Mulhouse complex illustrate how imported machinery, expertise, capital and local labor connected remote regions to global cotton.
- Successful early regions usually had prior textile production, putting-out networks, merchant / artisanal capital, workers with relevant skills and exposure to British competition. Mechanization often destroyed hand spinning first while temporarily expanding hand weaving, then moved both into factories.
- Small early mills could begin with modest capital and reinvest profits. Large merchant fortunes also entered manufacturing: the Boston Associates built integrated Waltham / Lowell mills with capital connected to Atlantic and slave-based commerce; comparable merchant entry occurred in Mexico, Russia, Alsace, Lombardy and Catalonia.
- Kinship and marriage networks pooled investment, technical knowledge and management. Alsatian Dollfus, Koechlin and Schlumberger families and organizations such as the Société Industrielle de Mulhouse converted economic cohesion into political influence.
- British yarn and cloth exports placed older manufacturers under severe pressure. Regions mechanized partly to substitute domestic production for imports; hand spinners’ falling wages and loss of work were a social cost of this competitive dynamic.
- Technology circulated by travel, journals, models, industrial espionage, artisan migration and, after 1843, legal machinery exports. British restrictions delayed but did not prevent diffusion; local machine builders soon copied and adapted equipment, dyes and processes.
- Beckert argues that machinery, capital, skills and older textile traditions were still insufficient. Durable industrialization required states able to protect borders and markets, subsidize / license enterprise, finance expertise, remove internal tariffs, build transport, enforce law, mobilize labor and sometimes purchase output.
- The Napoleonic Continental Blockade and U.S. embargo / wars temporarily shielded new mills. After 1815, manufacturers converted emergency protection into lasting tariffs in France, the United States, Prussia, Austria, Russia, Italian states, German customs unions and elsewhere.
- Mexico is a central non-European case: state loans through the Banco de Avío, foreign machinery and experts, tariff / import restrictions and political advocacy by Esteban de Antuñano supported rapid mill growth. Its partial domestic-market retention challenges narratives that industrialization was confined naturally to Europe.
- Colonial markets supported Dutch, Belgian, French, British and Catalan manufacturing; political loss of markets damaged Catalonia and Belgium. Swiss firms without colonies accessed imperial circuits through merchants and specialized products.
- Britain survived protection elsewhere by moving toward higher-quality goods and toward colonial / semicolonial markets whose governments could not exclude British imports. “Free trade” thus followed unequal political power rather than replacing it.
- Egypt under Muhammad Ali briefly created a large state-led industry using imported technology, monopolies and forced labor. Its reliance on coercion constrained domestic demand and wage-labor institutions; British pressure and the 1838 Anglo-Ottoman tariff undermined protection. The quoted European label “barbarian” must be framed as racist contemporary language.
- Brazil’s plantation elites prioritized slave-grown agricultural exports, low tariffs and labor allocation to agriculture; capital and credit remained tied to slavery. Early mills therefore remained marginal despite cotton, ports and some technical capacity.
- India underwent colonial deindustrialization because it lacked a government responsive to domestic manufacturers. The Pondicherry mill served French intercolonial trade, while sustained Indian-owned mechanization began later with Cowasji Nanabhoy Davar and post–Civil War cotton profits.
- The chapter can overstate a clean fit between state borders and industrialization. Its more defensible claim is that state capacity and the distribution of power inside states shaped whether entrepreneurs could protect markets and mobilize capital / labor.
- War capitalism enabled industrial capitalism through raw materials, markets and accumulated wealth, but slave / plantation regimes could impede domestic industrialization. Beckert’s concluding claim that slavery and colonial exploitation were “not essential” after diffusion should be treated as a proposition about institutional change, not as exoneration of capitalism’s continuing coercion.

## Chapter 7 — Mobilizing Industrial Labor

- Ellen Hootton’s 1833 testimony anchors the chapter in a child worker’s experience rather than an inventor’s biography. Her long day, unpaid “training,” beatings, iron weight and economic dependence show both direct bodily violence and the less visible compulsion of household poverty.
- Millions of workers entered humid, dusty, noisy and dangerous mills for machine-paced days commonly lasting twelve hours or more. The factory reorganized time, space, household strategies and bodily movement as radically as spinning technology reorganized production.
- Wage labor was historically constructed. Enclosure, decline of household spinning / weaving, loss of commons, weakened customary obligations, migration and state law reduced alternative access to subsistence and made labor power saleable.
- “Free” contracts retained penal coercion. Master-and-servant laws in Britain, Prussia and elsewhere allowed fines, prison and hard labor for breach; prisons, orphanages, workhouses and forced apprenticeships supplied mills. Employment at will emerged only after prolonged worker struggle.
- Early workers frequently moved between farming, household production and mills. High turnover and seasonal absence at Dover and elsewhere reveal attempts to preserve nonfactory livelihoods. Mill wages could temporarily help families remain on marginal land.
- Industrial capitalism depended on noncapitalist household reproduction. Employers located mills near poor rural districts and paid wages that assumed unpaid food production, child care and other family labor.
- Children were recruited because they were cheap, subordinated within households and institutions, and easier to discipline. Quarry Bank parish apprentices and widespread orphan / poorhouse transfers demonstrate that child labor was organized through public and familial authority, not simply individual choice.
- Women, especially young unmarried rural women, formed the majority in many cotton workforces. Historical spinning traditions, lower institutional wages and patriarchal authority made their labor central; this must not be naturalized as women’s aptitude or willingness.
- Beckert contrasts women’s mobility into European / North American factories with restrictions in China and parts of Asia / Africa. This large-scale gender comparison needs regional feminist labor-history verification and must avoid making patriarchy appear “favorable.”
- Gendered and child labor contributed to cotton history’s later invisibility compared with male-coded coal, iron and rail narratives. Observer William Rathbone’s comments on workers’ prettiness and respectability exemplify a male bourgeois gaze, not evidence of benign conditions.
- Factories used attendance books, fines, dismissal, dormitories, factory police, physical punishment and spatial confinement. Plantation accounting influenced industrial discipline, but wage labor and slavery remained legally and materially distinct regimes.
- Workers resisted by leaving, slowing work, destroying machinery, rioting, striking, forming unions and campaigning politically. Machine breaking in France, Britain, Switzerland, Mexico and elsewhere expressed rational defense of livelihoods, not technophobic ignorance.
- States criminalized unions, assemblies and machine breaking, deployed troops and built barracks in industrial districts. Capitalists’ reliance on repression shows the limits of private authority and makes labor control part of state formation.
- Collective action eventually nationalized worker claims as capital became tied to states. British, New England, Spanish, French, Silesian and other workers won gradual changes to contract penalties, hours and conditions; women organized the Lowell Female Labor Reform Association.
- Mill relocation to isolated factory villages along Catalan rivers shows how capital responded spatially to worker militancy, anticipating later global searches for more controllable labor.
- The chapter’s final synthesis joins enslaved plantation labor in the U.S. South to wage labor in European / New England mills through merchants and credit. Later memory separated the systems, obscuring slavery’s contribution to industrial capitalism.

## Chapter 8 — Making Cotton Global

- Liverpool is presented as the principal nineteenth-century junction where plantation slavery, industrial wage labor, finance, shipping and manufactured-goods markets met. Its supremacy rested on Atlantic slave-trade experience, proximity to Lancashire, docks, canals, railways and dense commercial institutions.
- Dock labor remained dangerous, casual and poorly paid. The exchange and merchants’ offices should not displace porters, sailors and migrants whose physical work moved bales through the system.
- Merchants were the “visible hand” coordinating a chain of factors, exporters, importers, selling / buying brokers, manufacturers, cloth exporters, local distributors and retailers. No single actor designed the whole network; local transactions collectively created a global production system.
- Commodity-chain position was unequal. Liverpool concentrated information, credit, standards and choice, while growers and consumers at the chain’s ends had fewer connections and less bargaining power.
- Dealers who owned stocks gave way to commission brokers who could compare all cotton in port. Brokers specialized, matched fiber properties to mill requirements, supplied information and arbitrated disputes; their institutions made high-volume trade possible.
- Trade shifted from identifiable bales and places toward samples, grades and standardized vocabulary such as “middling” and “fair.” Liverpool and New Orleans associations formalized duplicate standards. Nature’s variability was abstracted to meet machine production, but judgment and convention remained embedded in institutions.
- Bills of lading and “to arrive” contracts separated ownership from physical cotton and anticipated futures trading. Before the Civil War, most contracts still referred to deliverable cotton; full abstraction emerged later.
- Large import merchants such as Rathbone, Baring, Brown Brothers, Siegfried and Lecesne connected Liverpool, London, New York, New Orleans, Le Havre, Bremen and other ports. Quantitative market shares and profits demonstrate concentration but require independent verification.
- European and northern U.S. credit financed land purchases, enslaved people, supplies, transport and crop advances. Southern state bonds and plantation banks converted distant finance into territorial and slaveholding expansion.
- Enslaved people were collateral as well as laborers. Mortgages and defaults could put British / northern merchants in ownership of plantations and people; lobbying against legal restrictions made commercial complicity explicit.
- Factors translated world-market credit and mill demand into plantation expansion. They sold crops on commission, supplied provisions, arranged shipping, lent against future harvests and sometimes traded enslaved people.
- Credit made the system expandable but crisis-prone. Falling cotton prices or unpaid overseas debts could propagate through linked obligations, contributing to panics in 1825, 1837 and 1857.
- European capital penetrated slave plantations more readily than peasant cotton regions. In India, local cultivators and merchants retained control of production and interior trade; Indian exporters could exceed Europeans, and European houses sometimes borrowed Indian capital.
- Similar local commercial control persisted in China, Anatolia and Egypt, while Mexican mills acquired domestic cotton through regional networks. Mid-century cotton globalization was therefore substantial but incomplete and institutionally diverse.
- Information about creditworthiness, harvests, weather, wars, prices, qualities and consumer taste was a commercial asset. Letters, circulars, travel, samples, newspapers, telegraphy, chambers of commerce and broker associations accelerated and collectivized it.
- Mulhouse’s global cloth-sample collections show design knowledge operating inside trade: manufacturers recorded provenance, prices, colors and patterns to adapt production to distant consumers. This is a direct bridge from economic history to design-history questions of copying, classification and market-responsive form.
- Trust depended on family, friendship, religion, diaspora, ethnicity and place-based reputation. Greek, Armenian, Parsi and Jewish networks and firms such as Ralli and Volkart demonstrate “relational capitalism,” but these communities should not be stereotyped as naturally commercial.
- Kinship networks were gradually internalized in multi-office firms. Volkart Brothers joined Winterthur and Bombay, purchased raw cotton and sold European manufactures, foreshadowing vertically coordinated global trading companies.
- Merchants used collective political power to demand docks, railways, waterways, commercial law, enforceable agency, overseas market access and naval protection. Their shifting support for “free trade,” machinery restrictions or protection followed strategic interests rather than a consistent doctrine.
- Commercial law and state enforcement reduced reliance on personal trust while strengthening the states merchants lobbied. Merchant power thus helped construct institutions that could later diminish the intermediary’s relative importance.
- The chapter closes with a U.S. political contradiction: plantation elites needed continued slavery and territorial expansion, while northern industrialists increasingly favored a stronger national state, domestic industry and wage labor. Merchant diversification weakened the older North–South commercial alliance before the Civil War.

## Chapter 9 — A War Reverberates Around the World

- The U.S. Civil War exposed the cotton system’s dependence on slave-grown southern fiber. Confederate export prohibition followed by the Union blockade reduced exports from millions of bales to almost none, creating the first global industrial raw-material crisis.
- Stocks initially delayed the impact, but cotton prices rose sharply, mills shortened hours or closed, and workers in Britain, France, German states, Russia and elsewhere lost employment. Public relief, demonstrations, strikes, riots and troop preparation made raw-material supply a question of social order.
- Workers’ diaries and petitions record hunger and uncertainty; they should anchor the account alongside aggregate unemployment estimates. The global “cotton famine” did not affect all regions or product grades equally.
- Merchants holding cotton profited temporarily from scarcity and speculation. The crisis accelerated standardized forward contracts, published forward prices and a more formal futures market in Liverpool and Bombay.
- Manufacturers and governments sought substitute supplies in India, Egypt, Brazil, Anatolia, Algeria, Mexico, Peru, China, Central Asia, West Africa and Australia. Many schemes failed, but the search made cotton supply an explicit state and imperial project.
- In India, Manchester organizations demanded railways, roads, ports, irrigation, marketable land titles, penal contract enforcement and criminal sanctions for cotton adulteration. Colonial transport served both commodity movement and troop mobility.
- British policy reduced tariffs on imported manufactured cloth while encouraging raw-cotton exports, defining India’s colonial economic role against domestic mechanized industry. “Free trade” was made through fiscal, legal and infrastructural intervention.
- Officials and manufacturers disagreed over speed and risk after the 1857 rebellion, but both accepted deeper state penetration. The Economist’s analogy of India to a disabled child is colonial / racist rhetoric used to reconcile coercive intervention with laissez-faire doctrine; it requires explicit critical framing.
- High wartime prices diverted Indian cotton from domestic use and China, expanded acreage and made Bombay a major export center. Shorter staple and cleanliness forced mill adjustment, but Indian cotton prevented total European collapse.
- Egypt’s output expanded through canals, railways, gins and presses, but also coercion, large estates and additional enslaved Sudanese labor. Describing this only as modernization would erase its labor regime.
- Brazilian small cultivators shifted from subsistence crops to cotton as prices and credit rose. Similar price responses occurred elsewhere, while speculative claims that whole continents could be “cottonized” reveal imperial fantasy.
- European merchants and manufacturers often supported or considered recognizing the Confederacy to restore supply; Liverpool firms ran blockades, financed and equipped the South. Many workers, however, increasingly supported the Union and emancipation.
- Cotton diplomacy had limits: Britain also depended on northern grain, feared for Canada and weighed balance-of-power interests. Alternative supplies reduced the incentive for intervention.
- Enslaved people made emancipation by leaving plantations, withholding labor, supplying intelligence and serving in the Union army. The chapter rightly presents their action, not merely federal decree, as decisive.
- Post-emancipation observers feared freedpeople would choose subsistence and autonomy over plantation work. Caribbean experience and Morant Bay were read through racist assumptions that Black independence meant idleness or savagery.
- The Economist’s call to make nonwhite laborers obey white supervisors condenses the racial hierarchy of postslavery labor planning. Such passages are evidence of elite ideology, never an acceptable authorial vocabulary.
- The Civil War tested “free labor” cotton through wage labor, sharecropping, peasant cultivation, indenture and state coercion. Wartime output depended on exceptional prices, so long-term viability remained uncertain in 1865.
- Cotton interests pressed Reconstruction authorities to force freedpeople back to work and often opposed political rights. Economic recovery and racial domination were therefore intertwined.
- The global lesson was not simple market adaptation: states built law, infrastructure and coercive capacity to mobilize rural labor without formal ownership of people. Beckert calls this a transition toward new imperial forms of cotton capitalism.
- Peace caused cotton prices and speculative fortunes to collapse from Liverpool to Bombay. This synchronized panic demonstrates how deeply wartime crop choices, finance, real estate and information had linked distant regions.
- The chapter ends with “King Cotton” transformed rather than dethroned: slavery in the United States ended, but cotton capitalists and states had rehearsed new mechanisms for commanding land and labor globally.

## Chapter 10 — Global Reconstruction

- After emancipation, cotton capitalists treated labor as the central problem: world mill capacity kept expanding while the plantation system that had supplied it collapsed.
- Freedpeople demanded land, family autonomy, subsistence and control of work. Wartime “free labor” experiments and early postwar contracts instead tried to preserve plantation discipline through wages, overseers and compulsory yearlong service.
- Land restoration to former owners, Black Codes, vagrancy law and Freedmen’s Bureau pressure restricted mobility. The phrase “free and voluntary contracts” often concealed state compulsion and the closure of commons, hunting, grazing and other subsistence routes.
- Black citizenship, suffrage, labor withdrawal and political organization improved bargaining power. Sharecropping emerged as a contested compromise: it weakened gang supervision and preserved family work, but left land ownership and crop choice largely with landlords.
- Crop liens, high interest and merchant credit forced Black croppers to grow cotton and produced chronic debt. Redeemer violence, disenfranchisement, weak schooling, hostile courts and lynching later stripped away political leverage.
- White yeoman farmers also entered cotton through railways, stores, gins and credit; many lost land and became tenants. Convict leasing supplied another violently coerced labor force.
- U.S. cotton recovered rapidly, showing imperial observers that formal slavery could be replaced by sharecropping, tenancy, debt and state coercion.
- India, Egypt and Brazil expanded output while also building domestic mills. India’s exports shifted toward continental Europe and Japan, whose machinery could use shorter staple.
- Berar’s railway, telegraph, presses and Suez route joined Liverpool orders directly to cotton fields. British policy deliberately displaced hand spinning / weaving so labor and raw cotton would move into export agriculture.
- Egypt made exclusive private property from layered village claims, concentrated estates, fixed cultivators to land and financed irrigation through debt and forced labor. State bankruptcy and creditor control preceded British occupation in 1882.
- Surveys, property titles, cash taxes, lien / contract law and classification made land, crops and labor legible to states and creditors. “Waste land” conversion destroyed common grazing, forests and food resources.
- Cultivators rationally resisted unfamiliar seeds, wage farms and cotton specialization when local varieties, food crops and household markets were safer. Australia’s failure likewise showed that suitable land without cheap labor was insufficient.
- Sharecropping and family farming prevailed globally because cultivators resisted direct wage supervision and because unpaid household labor lowered costs. Women’s and children’s work must be made visible.
- Where land concentration was strongest, as in La Laguna, wage workers faced private police, jails, armed guards and physical punishment. Postslavery cotton remained coercive.
- The reconstruction fused state formation with global market making. Colonial officials and international cotton congresses exchanged techniques for producing cheap fiber through formally “free” labor.
- By the 1870s output and falling prices convinced manufacturers the supply crisis was solved; the result rested on the defeat or containment of cultivators’ land, political and subsistence claims.

## Chapter 11 — Destructions

- European and Japanese firms moved inland through railways, telegraphs, gins, presses and agencies. Vertically integrated houses such as Volkart replaced layers of local exporters, factors and brokers, while still relying on village moneylenders.
- Cotton exchanges, telegraphy, standardized “middling upland,” futures contracts and state statistics abstracted trade from particular bales and people. U.S. federal standards later displaced Liverpool’s private definitions.
- Colonial administrations deliberately turned raw-cotton regions into markets for machine cloth. Tariffs, transport and textile surveys—including sample books made for copying Indian products—joined commercial classification to design appropriation.
- Hand spinning and weaving declined unevenly. Domestic weaving persisted in India, China, the Ottoman world, Africa and Latin America by serving local taste, durability and niche markets, often through underpaid women’s and children’s household labor.
- Deindustrialization pushed millions, especially women, into agriculture or insecure work. It also undermined food security as cotton displaced grain and household production.
- Cash taxes, private property, debt, falling prices and drought exposed cultivators to global fluctuations. Famines in cotton-specialized India occurred amid continuing grain exports; famine photography must not be reproduced as spectacle.
- Cultivators resisted through riots against moneylenders and taxes, Populism, cooperatives, agrarian reform, workplace action and anticolonial “cotton nationalism.” These were informed responses to debt and political exclusion.

## Chapter 12 — The New Cotton Imperialism

- Japan’s Korean project, Russia’s Central Asian expansion and European African ventures sought “raw-material independence” for national industries. Industrial associations and states jointly researched, financed and administered colonial cotton.
- Territorial conquest, Indigenous dispossession, railways, irrigation, seeds, agronomy, tariffs and credit expanded cotton in Korea, Central Asia, Egypt, the U.S. West and Africa. No map material is eligible for the textbook.
- Cotton frontiers in Texas, Oklahoma and elsewhere continued Native removal. The book connects settler property rights and infrastructure to rapid acreage growth.
- Russia transformed Central Asia from a textile-producing region into a raw-cotton supplier and market for Russian cloth. Merchant advances, high interest, sharecropping, land loss and forced settlement produced dependence and food deficits.
- European projects in Africa frequently turned to compulsory cultivation, fixed prices, taxes, forced infrastructure labor and corporal punishment when cultivators preferred food crops or local textile markets.
- Tuskegee experts in German Togo transferred U.S. planting knowledge, but local ecology and Ewe knowledge exposed its limits. Booker T. Washington and John Robinson warned against cotton monoculture; German officials increasingly ignored them.
- Ewe mixed farming and higher-paying local spinners constrained export cotton. Subsistence access was active economic security, not “indolence.”
- Colonial policy sought deindustrialization explicitly: African spinners and weavers were to become export growers and consumers of European cloth.
- Togo, Congo, Mozambique and French territories reveal escalating coercion. Sexual violence, beating and compulsory sale must be treated as central evidence, not incidental excess.
- Imperial experts learned across borders even while pursuing national autonomy. The result was a transnational repertoire later used by Soviet, independent Indian and Chinese institutions.
- Cotton growers became politically marginal while nation-states and industrial capital strengthened. The global system remained interconnected, but the terms of incorporation deepened inequality and helped move manufacturing toward the global South.

## Chapter 13 — The Return of the Global South

- The chapter opens in Ahmedabad with Ranchhodlal Chhotalal’s Shapur Mill, founded in 1861. Its survival and expansion show that industrial cotton production outside the North Atlantic did not await the twentieth century; it grew from older merchant capital, caste and religious networks, regional textile knowledge and access to machinery.
- Vaishnav Vania and Jain financiers, merchants and millowners organized capital through dense local relations. The Gujarat Spinning and Weaving Company followed in 1877, and by 1918 Ahmedabad counted roughly fifty-one mills and thirty-five thousand workers. These figures are strong narrative anchors but must be checked against Indian industrial censuses and local mill records.
- Cotton manufacturing’s center of gravity gradually shifted. Britain’s share of global spindles fell from about 61 percent in 1860 to 43 percent in 1900 and 34 percent in 1930; its share of global cloth output had fallen further by the early 1930s. The precise denominator and series must be verified before textbook use.
- Organized labor in Britain, New England, continental Europe and other early industrial regions won shorter hours, higher wages and legal protections. National states had a double role: they secured property and markets for capital, yet workers could also use national political institutions to limit exploitation.
- As labor costs and regulation increased, manufacturers relocated or invested in lower-wage regions. Protectionist tariffs in Germany, Italy, France and the United States, together with imperial control over markets, made this geographical reorganization political rather than a neutral market adjustment.
- The U.S. South became a low-wage industrial zone within a national market. Cheap rural labor, weak labor law, child labor, racial disfranchisement and employer-friendly politics mattered more than proximity to cotton. Early mills recruited poor white families; later mills also drew Black former sharecroppers. The regional story must not be reduced to a racialized labor-cost comparison.
- Brazil’s cotton manufacturing expanded after abolition through tariffs, domestic demand and low-paid women’s and children’s labor. Its growth illustrates that the end of slavery could coincide with industrial expansion without eliminating coercive or gendered labor relations.
- Japan’s Meiji state imported machinery, trained engineers, sponsored model mills, supported merchant and industrial associations and used colonial expansion and war reparations to build industry. Manufacturers combined cheap Chinese cotton, two-shift production and extremely low-paid young women housed in dormitories.
- Japanese growth cannot be explained by culture or racial aptitude. State support, regional markets, engineering transfer, labor discipline and imperial power were decisive. Weak protective law, recruitment debts, confinement and worker absconding reveal the coercive conditions behind export success.
- In Egypt, colonial free-trade rules, taxation and political subordination constrained early industry. Nationalist capital centered on Bank Misr, Talʿat Harb and Misr Spinning later linked tariffs, industrialization and national independence.
- In India, Bombay and Ahmedabad mills grew from merchant capital and post–Civil War cotton profits. Colonial infrastructure and law facilitated machinery, transport and labor mobilization, but tariff and market policy continued to privilege Lancashire. Factory legislation was also shaped by Lancashire pressure, so “labor protection” and imperial competition cannot be separated.
- Millowners organized the Bombay Millowners’ Association, the Gujarat Industrial Association and related bodies to lobby over tariffs, rail rates, labor law and cotton quality. Capitalist collective action was thus as important as individual entrepreneurship.
- China retained large domestic textile traditions while treaty ports, imported cloth and foreign military power opened markets to outsiders. World War I temporarily reduced imports and enabled rapid mill growth; Japanese-owned mills, cheap labor and restored tariff autonomy later shaped competition.
- Chinese mills relied heavily on women, children, bonded labor and long hours. Limited state protection, foreign privileges and Kuomintang repression of labor weakened worker bargaining. These conditions require comparison with Chinese labor history rather than acceptance of generalized wage figures.
- Cotton became a vehicle of anticolonial politics in India. Excise taxes and discriminatory fiscal policy mobilized Tilak, Gokhale, Tata and other industrial and political actors; Swadeshi linked boycott, domestic manufacture and national identity.
- Gandhi’s hand-spinning and khadi politics gave cotton a different meaning: household labor, moral economy and mass mobilization opposed both imperial imports and industrial concentration. Yet Indian industrialists also demanded longer-staple cotton, grading standards, protected markets and the redirection of village production toward mills.
- Nationalist coalitions depended on workers and peasants, limiting capitalist autonomy. The Bombay Plan and subsequent state planning expressed a compromise in which industrialists accepted stronger public coordination to secure national development.
- Cotton workers were central to labor and anticolonial movements in Egypt, China and India. Independence brought nationalization, wage gains or new protections in some cases, but also labor repression when governments prioritized production and state-building.
- By the 1950s, state power and industrial capital were closely joined across much of the global South. From the 1970s, transnational capital began to loosen dependence on any single territorial state and acquired greater ability to relocate production.
- The chapter’s broad geographical comparisons reject racial or cultural essentialism, but they sometimes compress national and regional difference. Each textbook use must preserve the causal sequence—state, market, labor regime, technology and political struggle—rather than turn “the global South” into a single actor.

## Chapter 14 — The Weave and the Weft: An Epilogue

- The 1963 auction of the Liverpool Cotton Exchange’s furniture symbolizes the end of the city’s nineteenth-century command over cotton. Industrial buildings converted into museums, offices or apartments preserve material traces while often aestheticizing factory space and muting labor conflict.
- Plantation tourism can similarly foreground architecture and elite domestic life while marginalizing slavery. Heritage interpretation is therefore part of cotton history: preservation always selects which work, violence and agency remain visible.
- The epilogue’s quantitative picture of cotton production, manufacturing, trade and consumption reflects roughly 2012–2014. It is a historical snapshot, not current evidence. Every statistic, country ranking and corporate claim must be updated before use in a 2026 textbook.
- Cotton growing and textile manufacturing are again concentrated in Asia and other parts of the global South, while U.S. cotton remains deeply supported by federal programs. Synthetic fibers have surpassed cotton in global textile use, changing the material and environmental frame of the industry.
- Beckert discusses forced cotton production in Uzbekistan, the Aral Sea disaster, genetically modified seed debt and Indian farmer suicides. These are high-stakes claims with complex causal histories; they require current specialist research and must not be written as monocausal consequences of one technology or corporation.
- Since the 1970s, large retailers and apparel brands have increasingly coordinated “buyer-driven” chains. Retailers and branded clothing firms can compare suppliers across countries, impose price and delivery conditions and shift orders without owning most factories.
- Production states remain indispensable. Subsidies, farm programs, trade policy, labor regulation, repression and infrastructure still shape cotton chains. The argument is not that globalization erased the state, but that mobile capital can use differences among states to increase leverage.
- Greater mobility weakens workers’ bargaining where suppliers compete through wages, hours, safety and regulation. “Race to the bottom” should be treated as a mechanism to test in concrete sectors and periods, not a universal slogan.
- Cotton capitalism advances through repeated spatial recombination. Crisis in one configuration—slavery, civil war, labor militancy, colonial resistance, protection or decolonization—pushes capital, merchants and states to assemble land, labor, technology and finance elsewhere.
- The countryside remains central to industrial history. Plantations, farms, tenancy, debt and household reproduction are not a preindustrial background but conditions of factory production and global consumption.
- Beckert’s anti-Eurocentric conclusion asks readers to see nineteenth-century industrial expansion as catastrophe for many producers in Asia, Africa and the Americas, and twentieth-century decolonization as a major restructuring of capitalism rather than a peripheral political event.
- The closing moral claim holds together exploitation, violence, productivity and human creativity. The book does not present technological capacity as automatically emancipatory; justice depends on collective organization and political control over the gains and burdens of production.
- The epilogue includes photographs of contemporary retail and factory-disaster contexts. Image use requires independent rights clearance, full event context and a dignity assessment. All maps and map-like figures are excluded by the textbook’s standing rules.

## Notes, bibliography, index and source apparatus

- The notes occupy roughly characters 1,036,388–1,500,000 and provide a dense multilingual research apparatus rather than a short popular bibliography. The index and illustration credits extend to the end of the file.
- Beckert draws on archives in Britain, France, Germany, the United States, India, Egypt, Japan, Mexico, Argentina and other regions. Repositories include the British National Archives, British Library and India Office Records, Maharashtra State Archives, Manchester and Liverpool collections, Baring and Volkart business records, and multiple state, municipal and company archives.
- The secondary literature spans global history, slavery and capitalism, Indian textile history, Ottoman and Egyptian history, African and Latin American cotton, merchant networks, labor, imperial state formation and commodity-chain studies.
- This apparatus makes the book a strong P1 synthesis and a valuable locator of P0 materials. It does not convert every quoted number or archival paraphrase into P0 evidence: textbook claims must return to the cited archive, official series or later specialist study where feasible.
- The notes reveal frequent author calculations and reconciliations of noncomparable series. Dates, crop/output shares, wages, prices, market shares, worker numbers and “first” claims require verification of units, geographical scope and denominator.
- The index is useful for locating people, firms, regions and concepts, but it is not independent evidence. Its headings also reflect the author’s conceptual organization and should not be mistaken for neutral taxonomy.
- The full source apparatus confirms that the book’s global narrative was constructed through heterogeneous evidence—merchant letters, government reports, factory inquiries, newspapers, parliamentary debates, trade statistics and later scholarship. A textbook borrowing the narrative should preserve these evidentiary differences rather than flatten them into one omniscient voice.
