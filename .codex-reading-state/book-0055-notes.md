## Editorial origin and source type

- The book developed from a three-day Stanford workshop in summer 1992, organized by the Project on People, Computers, and Design with support from NSF, Interval Research Corporation, and the Association for Software Design.
- About thirty software, graphic, industrial and research practitioners participated. The book was developed through several years of prototyping, revision and user testing.
- It is an edited, multi-author intervention intended to establish software design as a reflective, user-oriented practice, not a single-author archival history and not a how-to rulebook.
- Authors include software designers, researchers, educators, industry managers and designers from architecture/graphic/product/urban fields.
- The book explicitly addresses practitioners, managers, HCI students/researchers, other design professions and software users.
- Because many cases are written by participants or affiliated practitioners, they are valuable actor accounts but require project archives, code, interface versions, business records, user studies and affected-user evidence for historical claims.

## Preface and Introduction, initial notes `[0, 30000)`

- “Software” is treated as ambiguous: program structure, application functionality, interface, and overall hardware-software user experience.
- “Design” links designer intent and activity to what people experience in practice across graphic, product and architectural disciplines.
- The book seeks cross-disciplinary lessons but explicitly calls itself exploratory rather than formulaic.
- It opposes treating software design as a hard-edged certainty and emphasizes human openness, context and use.
- The editors make users the most important group and argue that informed buyers/users can exert pressure for better software.
- Winograd calls the ideal reader a “reflective designer,” joining practical work with reflection on what works and why.
- The 1990 Kapor manifesto, 1992 Association for Software Design, 1994 *interactions*, 1994/1995 CHI design programming, attempted Microsoft–Intuit acquisition, and Mosaic/Netscape are presented as signs of software design’s professional emergence.
- Historical claims about dates, institutional founding, acquisition value, market effect and Internet popularization must be checked against ACM/ASD records, antitrust documents, software release histories and usage data.
- Software design is distinguished from but connected to programming, software engineering, software architecture, human factors and interface design.
- “Works” is broadened beyond functional specification, reliability and efficiency to include people, values, needs, results and satisfying experience.
- Software is framed as a medium that generates “virtualities” or worlds in which users inhabit, perceive and act; desktop, spreadsheet and Internet are treated as worlds, not merely device interfaces.
- The architectural analogy highlights how software shapes patterns of life, but it risks masking code ownership, institutional access, physical infrastructures and uneven ability to leave or modify the designed world.
- Device/interface design cannot be separated from software: PDAs, VR equipment, embedded devices, tactile/audio/wearable interfaces are treated as an emerging horizon in 1996.
- Mainstream HCI cognition is considered necessary but insufficient; aesthetic, affective and emotional experience is added, with games and entertainment as prominent precedents.
- Do not extrapolate 1996 predictions to contemporary platforms or AI. Treat terms such as “future,” “smart jewelry,” and wearable computing as period expectations.

## Introduction completed and Chapter 1 `[30000, 60125)`

- Winograd describes design as conscious but not reducible to universal method; intuition, tacit knowledge and judgment remain.
- Design keeps human concerns central while managing incommensurable tradeoffs. Ease of use is not a universal maximum: expert performance, disability access, children and other groups can require different choices.
- Designing is an iterative “conversation with materials” at the project and disciplinary levels. Digital interaction is presented as a medium with unusually rapid modification.
- Creativity includes finding or framing problems, not merely solving given ones. Studio/critique traditions are proposed as relevant educational models.
- Design communicates explicit content, use conventions, cultural meaning, emotion and aesthetic stance. Artifacts are interpreted through historical, physical and social context.
- Design has social consequences in workplaces; action-centered and participatory approaches are introduced as ways to expose organizational/political effects.
- Design is social and organizational, not an individual genius act. The book juxtaposes large-organization constraints with customer-oriented organizational support.
- Mitchell Kapor’s 1990 manifesto calls software design a professional peer to computer science and engineering and casts the designer as “champion of the user experience.”
- Kapor’s Lotus 1-2-3 work with programmer Jonathan Sachs exemplifies interactive division between conceptual/interaction design and implementation; authorship and roles require project archives.
- Kapor adapts Vitruvius’s firmness, commodity and delight to software: reliability, suitability and pleasurable experience.
- He argues that interface cannot be appended after product architecture; Dan Bricklin’s spreadsheet metaphor is his example of overall conceptual design.
- Proposed software-design curriculum: representation/composition, HCI and workplace research, technical systems, at least one programming language, history/precedent, media comparison, studio projects and development-process integration.
- HyperCard is praised for surface simulation but said to model behavior poorly; object-oriented components are proposed as a prototyping route. Treat this as a 1990 technology judgment.
- Kapor insists design and implementation must interact but retain distinct professional identities. His architecture-derived hierarchy gives a designer overall responsibility; this can obscure distributed authorship, programmer design work, labor hierarchy and user participation.
- Statistics such as “75 percent of modern program code deals with interface,” claims about the first curriculum, and the manifesto’s reception need original evidence.
- The manifesto is an actor intervention in professional boundary-making, not neutral proof that a separate occupation was established.

## Profile 1: Software Design and Architecture `[60125, 72368)`

- The profile critically examines rather than simply endorses the architecture analogy.
- It compares artisan-programmer, master architect-builder and separated architectural firm/implementation roles, while noting that most buildings globally do not follow a neat architect/contractor split.
- End-user and local software modification are compared to remodeling; the question becomes how software can be modifiable without losing “firmness.”
- Architectural education contributes study of genres/precedents, learning from disasters, studio critique and practical construction/economics/materials.
- Software genres such as graphic word processors are treated as historical/conventional systems, not isolated inventions.
- Architecture’s material components, spaces and experiences are mapped to code/technical construction, virtual spaces and user experience.
- The profile warns that the commissioning client may differ from inhabitants/end users.
- The analogy is explicitly heuristic; every similarity can produce a counterexample.
- Architectural “master designer” language must not be adopted without examining collective authorship, legal liability, licensing, maintenance, accessibility and labor.

## Chapter 2: David Liddle interview and Xerox Star `[72368, 101476)`

- Liddle defines software design as determining the user’s whole experience and privileges the user’s conceptual model over information display and command machinery.
- Historical chain presented: Engelbart/SRI; Alan Kay’s Dynabook; PARC Alto, Ethernet and laser printing; 1975 platform/Pilot/Mesa development; 1978 product-line commission; May 1981 Star launch.
- Star process begins with task analysis and scenarios, then distinguishes information display, control/command mechanisms and conceptual model.
- Metaphors are treated as abstractions that support work and recognition, not literal imitation of physical desktops or accounting pads.
- Reported evidence: a roughly 400-page functional specification; iterative mockups/prototypes/specification; Bill Verplank’s 600–700 hours of synchronized user/screen/hand video; two-button mouse selected from testing.
- Star concepts include object-specific property sheets, progressive disclosure, direct manipulation, WYSIWYG, generic Move/Copy/Delete/Open/Properties commands and cross-application consistency.
- Liddle criticizes application icons and Unix/DOS retrofits in Macintosh, Windows and NeXT; these are participant judgments, not neutral comparative tests.
- User-first design produced conflict and rework and reportedly contributed to slow Version 1.0 performance.
- Corporate history: repeated cancellation threats, lack of manufacturing/marketing owner, Don Massaro’s late support, reported nearly 100,000 Star/Viewpoint shipments, and failure to build an open third-party application platform.
- Integrated/closed system is presented as both design strength and market weakness.
- Liddle gives Star a “first systematic and extensive prototyping cycle” claim. This must be treated as a priority claim requiring earlier software/HCI project evidence.
- His three-phase technology adoption model—enthusiast, business productivity, discretionary consumer—is a period generalization, not an established universal sequence.
- “Software design has nothing to do with how code works inside” conflicts with the book’s own insistence on material/technical constraints; retain as Liddle’s polemical boundary claim.

## Profile 2: Alto and Star `[101476, 110572)`

- PARC is presented as the 1970 institutional setting; Alto is dated 1972 and credited to Kay’s Learning Research Group plus Computer Systems Laboratory under Robert Taylor.
- Profile links bitmap display, mouse, removable storage and single-user multitasking with Smalltalk, Bravo, Draw/Markup, Ethernet file services and laser printing.
- Alto’s hypothetical standard-markup price is reported above USD 75,000; financial basis requires archive verification.
- Star targeted office users without prior computer expertise and standardized direct manipulation, WYSIWYG and generic commands.
- CUSP scripting and early internationalization/non-left-to-right/large character sets complicate an exclusively English GUI story; language-version archives are needed.
- Claims that laser printing/networking/desktop publishing were “first developed at PARC,” and genealogy from Bravo to Word or Draw/Markup to later products, need patent, code and personnel archives.

## Chapter 3 opening `[110572, 120000)`

- Gillian Crampton Smith and Philip Tabor argue that artist-designers are not decorators; they work with implicit social, functional, cultural and emotional meaning.
- Interaction design’s product is experiential. Their slogan “WYGIWYS: what you get is what you see” rejects a hierarchy of underlying function over appearance.
- Software is described as an everyday cultural environment and symbolic consumer object; attention competition and richer displays increase visual/auditory meaning.
- Interpretation depends on context, cultural codes, emotions and prejudices. Engineering-first/visual-later is rejected.
- Proposed artist-designer capacities: invention, empathy, evaluation and visualization/representation.
- Artist/engineer distinctions are said to converge through teamwork, though claims about temperament and specialization risk naturalizing occupational categories.

## Chapter 3 completed `[120000, 149554)`

- Graphic design is defined as finding useful structure in information and giving it visual form so that explicit and implicit meanings communicate to an audience.
- Sparse formal means—frame, text, line/shape/image and empty space—make small distinctions, inherited styles and changing cultural associations important.
- Form/content and function/aesthetics are described as inseparable. A railway timetable communicates an institutional affect of dependability as well as departure data; this implied trust may be untrue.
- Templates, guidelines and perceptual principles are useful craft resources but cannot generate a context-appropriate design without invention and pragmatic balancing.
- The chapter resists late “spray-on aesthetics”; artist-designers, marketers, accountants and engineers all lose influence when brought in after core decisions.
- Five recursive activities form the interaction-design core: understanding, abstracting, structuring, representing and detailing.
- Accounts-package case: accountant/software engineer encoded paper procedures before interaction design; late visual cleanup could not correct an obsolete conceptual model.
- Suitcase student project: students analyzed available versus installed fonts, then moved font management from a separate desk accessory into the application menu, using terminology, animation and visible state.
- Fashion-manufacturing project: observation across factories/studios exposed divergent departmental views; the garment became the central object; fifty paper representations were compressed into portfolio and timeline views.
- The team used sketches, a cut-up “map,” action alerts, direct desktop notification, garment images and schedule bars. Approximate labor is given as two full-time plus three part-time participants over four months, about one person-year.
- The fashion system appears to be a research/design proposal; implementation, workplace adoption, worker response, surveillance effects and production outcomes are not established.
- The chapter concludes interaction design concerns purpose, conceptual/representational model and detailed look/feel. It calls the field more art than science because meanings and preferences resist universal prediction.
- “Empathy” and designer hunch must not substitute for participation, accessibility testing or affected-worker evidence.

## Profile 3: Kid Pix `[150444, 156375)`

- Kid Pix is presented as a children’s drawing program whose main function is playful activity rather than preservation/printing of drawings.
- Eight full-screen erasers produce the same end state through different animation and sound, deliberately violating reductive consistency to support delight.
- Profile credits Craig Hickman and traces functional genealogy through MacPaint/MacDraw and Alto paint programs.
- Popularity and “best-selling Macintosh application” are unsourced market claims requiring sales/platform data; children are inferred as audience rather than quoted as research participants.
- The claim that a cute spreadsheet would fail naturalizes a 1990s business aesthetic; use as actor judgment, not timeless rule.

## Chapter 4: Design Languages `[156375, 191466)`

- Rheinfrank and Evenson define design languages as evolving collections of elements, compositional principles and context-sensitive qualifications used for generation and interpretation.
- Languages are often tacit, historically assimilated and co-produced through use; conscious development can accelerate change.
- Nantucket houses illustrate a language arising from climate, cedar material, fishing/whaling economy and piecemeal expansion, later codified after original necessities changed. The account omits Indigenous land, resource and labor history.
- Xerox copier example uses handles, frontal orientation, color/value coding and point-of-need graphics across a product family.
- Meaning is not built into form alone; objects participate in situated sense-making and languages evolve through use, failure, invention and accident.
- Digital camera, cellular communicator, My First Sony, Nike and Reebok examples connect design language with corporate strategy. These are consultancy claims and period market forecasts, requiring independent product, consumer and business evidence.
- “Transparent-box” design selectively reveals functions and supports learning; “black-box” simplicity can lower barriers while de-skilling or limiting community practice. The chapter acknowledges the gain/loss tension.
- Explicit consistency can support transfer across product families, but corporate design language also aims at market dominance and customer lock-in.
- Five-step language process:
  1. characterization of inherited assumptions;
  2. reregistration through field research and alternative frames;
  3. simultaneous language development and concrete demonstrations;
  4. early evaluation in contexts of use;
  5. continuing evolution and user modification.
- FRS process-control case reframes the control room from remote machinery panel to communication/business hub. Operators, engineers, maintenance, quality, planning, sales and executives are proposed as contributors.
- Prototype evaluation reveals that “people buttons” beside power buttons confused users, demonstrating collision between visual similarity and different action semantics.
- The chapter criticizes WIMP as an historically contingent 1980s language and anticipates different game/CD interaction forms.
- Field-research and coevolution rhetoric is important, but employer/client power, safety regulation, worker bargaining, plant surveillance and actual deployment outcomes are not documented.

## Profile 4: Macintosh Human Interface Guidelines `[191466, 199103)`

- Macintosh ease-of-use is attributed to GUI plus cross-application consistency, Apple evangelists, published 1987 guidelines and a programming toolbox that made compliance easier than violation.
- Standard menu terms, dialog types, windows, clipboard and cut/copy/paste establish a design language across independent developers.
- Profile acknowledges that the clipboard may have been less functional than Star’s Move/Copy/Delete scheme; consistency rather than technical superiority drove learning transfer.
- Claims that GUI caused early Macintosh success, or that consistency took “many years” to reach PC/Unix, require sales, user studies, version histories and competing-interface evidence.
- Guidelines both coordinate and discipline third-party authors; future study should include developer resistance, platform control, accessibility and localization.

## Chapter 5 opening `[199103, 210000)`

- Paul Saffo disputes a simple equation between user-friendliness and market success.
- DOS PCs, VCRs and consumer-electronics failures are used to argue that task importance, alternatives, cultural learning and price matter.
- “Threshold of indignation” denotes the behavioral compromise a person will accept before perceived hassle outweighs task value.
- Workplace users may be compelled to tolerate harmful or difficult systems; business training can raise thresholds. This is power/compulsion, not voluntary consumer preference.
- Repetitive-strain injury and CRT eyestrain appear as costs, but the chapter does not develop occupational health governance.
- Age-cohort claims about ATMs and the Web are broad stereotypes without sample evidence; they require demographic, disability, income and access analysis.

## Chapter 5 completed `[210000, 223472)`

- Saffo maps products/users by threshold of indignation and willingness/ability to pay.
- Four caricatures—Air Force general, teenage hacker, Hollywood starlet and average consumer—are rhetorically memorable but reproduce gender, age, class and occupational stereotypes; they are not valid user segments for textbook adoption.
- Business versus consumer devices are placed on a continuum of cost, complexity and control. The useful insight is that organizational permission/bureaucracy shapes apparent usability.
- Family example assumes parents buy and technically tolerant children install equipment; this naturalizes age and household roles.
- Claimed trends: falling component cost/performance improvement moves products toward consumer use; cultural learning raises tolerance; firms have difficulty changing market zone; adaptive interfaces should reveal capability with skill; advertising/hype can increase willingness to try; adjacent-market movement is safer.
- The chapter’s version of Moore’s law (“factor of 10 every 2.5 years” for cost and circuits) needs correction against Moore’s original statements and semiconductor data.
- Claims that extra computing power necessarily produces user-friendliness, thresholds steadily rise, or consumer electronics would win “new media” are deterministic 1990s forecasts.
- “Adaptive interfaces” introduces progressive skill-sensitive disclosure but does not address profiling errors, user control or accessibility.
- Useful textbook role: a period industry-forecast document showing how usability was joined to price, compulsion, culture and corporate capability—not a validated consumer-adoption model.

## Profile 5: Mosaic and the World Wide Web `[223472, 231324)`

- Profile credits Tim Berners-Lee/CERN with early-1990s WWW development for research communication and Marc Andreessen/NCSA colleagues with Mosaic.
- It presents 1993 network use as expert-heavy and 1994 as a mass-access shift driven largely by Mosaic.
- Key arguments: critical mass, simple HTML/HTTP, distributed open development and network software distribution.
- Simplicity omitted security and formatting features, lowering development barriers; later growth increased standards complexity.
- Free federally funded distribution and direct downloading supported rapid spread; Netscape is said to establish a de facto standard within weeks.
- The profile’s “first cross-server hypertext,” millions of users, rapid causal impact and Netscape timing are strong claims requiring CERN/NCSA code, release logs, server/browser statistics, standards records and histories including ViolaWWW, Lynx and other browsers.
- “Open development” is described as grassroots evolutionary selection, but institutional funding, governance, standards power, commercial enclosure, accessibility, labor and global infrastructure are underdeveloped.

## Chapter 6: Action-Centered Design `[231324, 259125)`

- Denning and Dargan criticize waterfall/spiral and specification-centered engineering for blindness to users’ domains of action.
- They distinguish software engineering assumptions—product, derivation from specification, limited client contact—from human-centered assumptions—satisfied customer, collaboration, evolving specification, continuous communication.
- The 1979 GAO nine-project statistic (2% used, 25% undelivered, 50% delivered/unused) is second-hand through Neumann and must be checked in the original report, project definitions and budget categories.
- They propose action-centered design as a bridge between system formalism and situated human action, using software architecture as the professional label.
- Interviews with designers of Quicken, MeetingMaker, Synchronize, Topic and Macintosh reportedly found a shared approach: choose breakdown-rich domain; study repeated actions/complaints/desires; imitate familiar patterns and enable desired new actions; deploy early prototypes; maintain customer communication.
- This is an award-winning/successful-case sample without described protocol or failed-case comparison. Claims of unanimity and causal success require original interview materials.
- Some designers reportedly ignored standard software engineering and used patch/rewrite cycles. This is actor evidence, not endorsement of poor maintainability or safety.
- “Customer satisfaction” narrows the affected population to a purchasing/client relation and can omit workers, nonusers, public harms and coercion.
- Pattern mapping draws from Christopher Alexander. Proposed maps record linguistic distinctions, speech acts, standard practices, tools, breakdowns and concerns, linking domain ontology to software structures and measures.
- Action-centered design emphasizes request/promise/completion/satisfaction language. It risks forcing ambiguous, collective or contested work into a managerial commitment ontology.
- The authors explicitly call the method preliminary, not a final answer.

## Profile 6: Business-Process Mapping opening `[259125, 270000)`

- Technology introduction reorganizes work; business-process redesign is placed before software automation.
- Three levels are distinguished: material processes, information processes and business processes.
- Business processes are modeled as networks of commitments and language actions rather than form/data routing.
- ActionWorkflow transaction has request, negotiation, performance and completion between “customer” and “performer”; subsidiary requests form a network.
- Work mapping uses interviews/observation to represent acts, roles, satisfaction conditions and incompletions.
- This model can reveal gaps and repeated breakdowns, but treating all work as customer/performer loops may hide hierarchy, refusal, informal care, conflict, collective bargaining and uncompensated work.

## Profile 6 completed `[270000, 271171)`

- Proposed supports include task-linked tools/information, reminders, alerts, workflow history, staff and manager overviews, and procedure automation.
- Such visibility may improve coordination but also enables managerial monitoring, pace control and performance surveillance; the profile does not analyze these consequences.
- Applicability beyond commercial business is claimed broadly. Public, care, educational and collective work require models of rights, discretion and non-market accountability.

## Chapter 7: Keeping It Simple `[271171, 305520)`

- Brown and Duguid argue that clarity can come from peripheral material/social resources, not fuller explicit representation.
- A book’s binding, typography, page structure and genre tell readers how to interpret text before its propositional content.
- Answering-machine hiss, recorded voice and conventions make “I’m not here now” intelligible; changing recording quality can remove a once-useful clue.
- Digital-library critique: extracting sentences from books can strip location, edition, genre, authority and material cues needed to assess how information matters.
- George Washington quotation examples show that the same sentence functions differently in biography, satire, ideology or children’s reference.
- Newspaper argument: finite page, editorial selection, location, simultaneous circulation and shared readership help make events “news.” Personalized database selection can lose public/common significance.
- Knight-Ridder Viewtron’s reported USD 50 million loss is used against easy medium replacement; amount and causal interpretation need corporate/industry records.
- “Border” names those stable parts of context that travel with mobile artifacts; publisher-designed covers became a shared genre resource after standardized edition binding.
- Genre supports shared interpretation, lets communication leave more implicit, and can guide information retrieval. Genre can also be manipulated through junk mail, advertising or bogus software.
- Innovation may cross genre boundaries; hip hop sampling, hypertext, zines, video and jazz are examples. Their treatment is brief and risks extracting cultural practices—especially Black music—into a generalized innovation metaphor without creators, rights or political context.
- Program/content and software/other-design boundaries blur in games, MOOs, HTML and SGML; designers need to develop new interpretive conventions.
- Central claim: future simplicity comes from underrepresentation and socially learned implicit resources, not maximal explicitness.
- Critical limit: implicit cues can exclude newcomers, disabled users, cross-cultural readers and those outside a dominant genre community. Textbook use must pair contextual richness with accessibility, provenance and explicit recourse.

## Profile 7: Microsoft Bob `[306441, 314836)`

- Released early 1995 for novice home users, Bob used a cartoon domestic room, familiar object metaphors, selectable characters, personality-tuned messages, limited applications and a coordinated product name, magazine, packaging and advertising campaign.
- Profile interprets Bob as a deliberate attempt to create a non-office software genre and to design the product periphery as part of user experience.
- Domestic familiarity is treated as universal and uncomplicated; this ignores household diversity, gendered domestic labor, disability, literacy and cross-cultural meanings.
- Cartoon agents draw on Nass/Reeves media-equation research. User response, sales, abandonment and later reputation are not provided.
- The profile calls Bob a notably large conscious orchestration but does not establish success. It is especially valuable as a 1996 contemporaneous reading before later “failure” canonization.

## Chapter 8: Designer’s Stance `[314836, 343400)`

- David Kelley contrasts engineering problem solving with design creation beyond a given brief, while repeatedly acknowledging that good engineers also use design intuition.
- Design is characterized as messy, ambiguous and dependent on point of view; drunk driving demonstrates that problem framing varies among drivers, police, bartenders and activists.
- Kelley attributes intuition to accumulated experience and argues creativity can be trained through openness, improvisation, risk and hands-on experience.
- Three activities: understand, observe and visualize. The crucial “leap” decides what the thing will be before implementation.
- Studio environment should make experiments and spectacular failure acceptable, but real safety, public and labor consequences limit which failures can be celebrated.
- Multidisciplinary teams broaden alternatives; later narrowing may use an emergent “strong, fair leader” or a user council.
- Kelley’s “25 insightful homemakers” toaster council and IDEO character types reproduce gender/class/user stereotypes and do not describe representative or empowered participation.
- Steve Jobs and da Vinci are invoked as heroic confident visionaries; this can re-center individual genius despite the chapter’s team claims.
- IDEO’s brainstorm norm disperses idea ownership and rewards reciprocal participation. It may support collective authorship, but the text does not address credit, intellectual property, working hours or power to decline.
- Broad exploratory research before manufacturing is justified through a 10× cost progression; this numerical ratio is a consultancy heuristic without supplied evidence.
- Design methodology is treated as reflective scaffolding, not a fixed recipe. Companies should remain mindful without believing the process can be standardized completely.
- Actor/interview source: use to study IDEO/Stanford professional ideology, not as universal proof of creativity or organizational effectiveness.

## Profile 8: IDEO `[344098, 352985)`

- IDEO is described as a merger of David Kelley Design and Bill Moggridge’s ID2, founded 1979; ID2 is said to have coined “interaction design” in the 1980s. The priority claim needs term-history verification.
- Five interdependent steps: understand; observe; visualize/predict; evaluate/refine; implement.
- Character maps, scenarios and storyboards concretize anticipated use; cross-functional work keeps technology, aesthetics, manufacturability, cost, durability and maintenance visible.
- Fictional automobile personas include “investment banker,” “confident” ranch owner, “set in ways” retirees and “reckless” young couple. They reveal rather than solve stereotyping risk.
- Multiple three/four/seven-step variants confirm that methodology is a reference structure.
- Claims that IDEO was among the world’s largest/most successful, coined a term, created Apple’s first mouse and integrated cultures require company/client archives and alternative contributor accounts.

## Chapter 9 opening `[352985, 360000)`

- Donald Schön distinguishes knowing-in-action, reflection-in-action during surprising performance, reflection-on-action through pause/reframing, and reflection-on-practice across repeated projects.
- Jazz improvisation and architectural sketching illustrate continuing action while interpreting unintended consequences.
- The “materials talk back” formulation is metaphorical: drawings, prototypes and code make consequences perceptible; artifacts do not possess literal agency.

## Chapter 9 completed `[360000, 380093)`

- Design complexity means every move has unintended as well as intended effects; means, ends and problem framing evolve together.
- Gillette double-honing/silicone example and 3M Scotch Tape differentiation illustrate new problems and uses emerging after production. Dates, naming etymology and reported 20–30 businesses need corporate/patent records.
- “Conversation with users” includes observation of behavior, not only speech. Users can redefine what a product is.
- Polanyi’s tacit dimension explains how mechanisms recede during skilled use and become visible in breakdown.
- MIT Project Athena cases contrast McCavity tutor (students bored/subverted it for answers) with Growltiger structural simulation (surprises prompted inquiry).
- The contrast comes from limited cases/interviews. It does not establish that open simulation always teaches better than tutoring.
- Design “taste” is a learned capacity to discriminate quality and to judge one’s own work. Schön’s teacher/student “gyroscope” still places authority in a critic’s judgment; cultural difference and accessibility can be mislabeled lack of taste.
- Student bicycle, sugar bowl and pen discussions show holistic personal attachment cannot be reduced to component attributes.

## Profile 9: Apple Interface Design Project and education `[380093, 389432)`

- Joy Mountford initiated Apple Computer Interface Design Project in 1991: invited universities, equipment grants, Apple liaison, interdisciplinary teams, iterative prototypes, representative-user testing, and video/written documentation of social/commercial/educational aspects.
- Reported five-year participation spans US, Canada, Netherlands, India, Japan, Sweden, Australia and UK, including technical and art/design institutions.
- Stanford PenPal 1994 student prototype integrated children’s image/sound/drawing and Internet sharing; no implementation/adoption outcome is claimed.
- Three programs:
  - Royal College of Art Computer-Related Design, initiated 1989, master’s studio across design, engineering and psychology;
  - Stanford HCID within computer science/product design, with Silicon Valley mentors;
  - MIT Media Arts and Sciences Software Design Studio, critique cycles and prototype jury.
- This is valuable evidence for 1990s HCI/interdisciplinary studio pedagogy, but comes from organizers/participants and requires curricula, enrollment, student work, institutional records and nonelite school comparisons.
- It offers no evidence on Vkhutemas or Chinese design education policy/institutions.
- Apple equipment grants and liaison raise questions about platform influence, corporate recruitment, intellectual property and who could participate.

## Chapter 10: Cultures of Prototyping `[389432, 415235)`

- Michael Schrage treats prototype media, timing, participants, ownership and organizational politics as a “prototyping culture.”
- Prototypes and specifications are competing/complementary representations; prototypes can invalidate requirements or expose insufficient ambition.
- Prototype-driven specifications are advocated against long specification-first work, but high-risk/safety-critical systems may require formal verification as well as iteration.
- Clay/CAD automobile comparison says US clay as input slowed iteration while Toyota used CAD then output clay; reported 40-day quarter-scale model and competitiveness interpretation require company/project evidence.
- Prototype choice should follow the question. Simple questions may need sophisticated prototypes and vice versa; unasked questions matter.
- Toothpaste “suckback” shows prototypes can create shared vocabulary and calibration.
- Speed/cycle claims compare Sony, 3M, Microsoft, LSI Logic, IBM, AT&T, GM and J&J largely through interviews/consultancy observation.
- Claim that more prototypes per unit time produce a more polished product is not causally established and may encourage speed/overwork or superficial metrics.
- Prototypes mediate executive persuasion, internal secrecy, premature shipping, departmental power and customer/supplier participation.
- “Community property” challenges engineering/marketing ownership, but does not settle credit, confidentiality, consent or liability.
- Reported cases—Nike/athletes, IBM AS/400 customers, Microsoft application firms, Intuit beta users—need contracts, participant selection and outcome data.
- Periodic prototyping at Honda (10–12 builds at two-week intervals) and Motorola Bandit is treated as measurable progress; quantity cannot replace relevance/safety/worker conditions.
- Mapping who sees, changes, requests and ignores prototype modifications is a useful power-analysis method.
- Schrage explicitly says the great ethnographies “have yet to be written,” while also calling culture/product relationships demonstrably clear; this evidentiary tension must be retained.

## Profile 10: HyperCard, Director and Visual Basic `[415235, 430821)`

- HyperCard (Apple, 1987) uses card-stack metaphor, direct interface building and English-like HyperTalk; it was bundled free and used from schools to games/prototypes.
- Director uses stage/score/cast, Lingo, timed animation, sound/video and higher-end hardware; suited to polished façade/kiosk prototypes more than complex computation.
- Visual Basic couples Basic with direct-manipulation Windows controls for professional developers and deliverable executables.
- The profile contrasts amateur/playful, artist-designer/multimedia and engineering/efficient cultures, then explicitly calls the characterization simplistic.
- Tool affordances, installed hardware, examples, team skill and organization shape outcomes. Period claims about popularity, price, school use, performance and typical aesthetics require release/usage evidence.

## Chapter 11 opening `[430821, 450000)`

- Shahaf Gal calls simulations and representations “image footholds”: temporary stable states that summarize prior moves and enable the next.
- Case is Ray, one MIT senior mechanical-engineering student in a three-week 1988 bridge contest with sixteen competitors.
- Ray begins top-down through precedent sketches, aesthetics, cable arrangements and time constraints, then becomes stuck.
- Growltiger comparison of six bridge types reveals unexpected girder strength and narrows alternatives.
- Physical laboratory tests reveal material/glue/string behavior that the simulation cannot represent, prompting bottom-up/component reasoning.
- Case use must preserve its small educational setting and avoid universalizing one student’s reflective account.

## Chapter 11 completed `[450000, 453697)`

- Ray’s final triple-deck/string design integrates material availability, vertical tensile members, construction and structural analysis; last-minute trusses were added.
- Bridge passed first load, failed when both deck ends crushed in second, and ranked fourth. The failure is essential evidence against a triumph-only narrative.
- Growltiger bounded the design frame as well as enabling exploration; physical tests and available materials also shaped the result.
- “Foothold” denotes a provisional representation for pause, comparison, orientation and later revision.
- Software tools should preserve past steps and support next moves without replacing material experiments or constraining creativity invisibly.

## Profile 11: Spreadsheet `[453697, 462554)`

- VisiCalc is credited with an interactive grid conceptual model; Lotus 1-2-3 adds refinement/IBM PC implementation and later macros.
- Profile distinguishes earlier ledger-like mainframe output from interactive recalculation of formulas and rapid “what-if” financial modeling.
- Spreadsheet combines familiar ledger form with interlinked formulas, making program/data and programmer/accountant boundaries more permeable.
- Macro capability supports “superusers” and local experts who create tools for colleagues.
- Claimed causal language—Lotus as “killer app,” spreadsheets leading PC revolution, “millions of offices,” productivity flourishing—requires sales, workplace, labor and alternative software evidence.
- Spreadsheet histories must include accountants, clerical workers, templates, error propagation, auditability, gendered office labor and organizational control, not only Bricklin/Kapor inventor genealogy.
- Key transferable ideas: representational virtuality, interactive modification and incremental end-user programmability.

## Chapter 12: Design as Practiced `[462704, 493055)`

- Donald Norman’s Apple power-switch case shows how a small usability issue expands into organizational, compatibility, cost, localization, safety, accessibility and technical constraints.
- Trigger: Macintosh 610 button under floppy slot was mistaken for eject, causing shutdown/data-loss risk.
- Multiple divisions—Entry, Desktop, Portables, Business Systems—had different cost, space, power and security priorities; central industrial design, dispersed human-interface groups, localization, safety, disability access and User Experience office also participated.
- Roughly thirty people, three months and about ten drafts are reported. Numbers and exact chronology require Apple committee records.
- Soft shutdown protects files but needs hard/emergency shutdown after crashes, sleep/awake, debug entry and full power disconnection.
- European symbol standards, multilingual labels, indicator lights, keyboard button and rear switch interact; the final compromise uses long press/double switch and optional reset.
- Fifty-cent cost as prohibitive and other numeric claims need product cost/sales evidence.
- Backward compatibility and Macintosh “ease of use” culture silently constrain possible questions. Even empowered engineers designing the user side can create inconsistency.
- Decentralized groups adapt locally but fragment product language; centralization coordinates but creates bureaucracy.
- Pending Apple/IBM CHRP standards destabilized the solution, showing design remains open to interorganizational platforms.
- Norman’s prescription: when a problem is posed, investigate the business model, organizational structure and culture that produced it.
- This is a candid participant account, but still a vice-president’s organizational view; users, engineers, customer support, accessibility staff and manufacturing workers are mostly represented indirectly.

## Profile 12: The Design of Everyday Things `[493055, 499767)`

- Profile summarizes affordances, natural versus arbitrary mappings, people-centered allocation of strengths and criticism of blaming users.
- Cooktop control layout, doors, sinks and locked car keys are used to show how physical form can reduce memory and error.
- “Newer cars” alerts/locking examples are period claims, not universal accessibility solutions.

## Chapter 13 opening `[499767, 510000)`

- Laura De Young proposes three organizational objectives: clear/shared goals, customer focus and designer empowerment.
- ManuSoft pseudonymous case contrasts management’s sufficient supporting finance product with designer Jane’s expansive “best” product; first prototype after eighteen months reveals misalignment and resource conflict.
- FinanceCo prototype case shows “28 screens” replacing the actual goal of communicating a concept.

## Chapter 13 completed `[510000, 532343)`

- Intuit is the positive actor case: goals begin with customer benefits/delivery and are repeated in project documents.
- Customer focus requires market research, direct observation, usability, QA and support rather than attitude alone.
- DataSoft color-display case illustrates decisions made without checking customer hardware/plans.
- Intuit’s “call ten customers” is explicitly not scientific sampling; it is a low-cost question-refinement method requiring judgment.
- Financial-calendar focus groups specifically mention women managing family finances. This may reveal gendered financial labor, but the chapter risks treating it as a natural female cognitive pattern.
- Follow-Me-Home observes initial home installation and exposed `Register` ambiguity; it is not equivalent to worker/user decision power and should not automatically be labeled participatory design.
- Quicken use by small business reportedly led to QuickBooks after renewed research; accounting accessibility is framed around avoiding double-entry expertise.
- Designer empowerment includes easy access to standards, competitor products, current equipment, design decision authority and balanced constraints.
- Intuit’s equal profit sharing/no sales commission is presented as reducing idea ownership; compensation, job class and actual distribution require company records.
- Final section recognizes layoffs, canceled projects, burnout and emotional engagement. It criticizes an organization that disbanded a successful training-product team and laid off half its members.
- Long hours are initially described as dedication, then loss of meaning as disempowerment. A labor-critical reading must reject overwork as the measure of commitment.
- The chapter is based on consultant interviews and Intuit self-description; it needs employees, laid-off workers, customer samples, contracts and performance data.

## Profile 13: Quicken `[532343, 543076)`

- Intuit founded 1984; Quicken released 1986. Scott Cook’s P&G/marketing background and household phone calls/user comparisons are presented as origins.
- Paper check/register graphics, QuickFill, scheduled entries and contextual calculator embody familiar-action plus detailed-support design.
- Claims: hundreds of calls, more than seven million users, Windows version in ten months/three weeks after Money, 60% share, USD 15 competitor price, 1995 USD 1.5 billion acquisition blocked on antitrust grounds.
- All market/acquisition figures, exact release chronology and causality require FTC/DOJ/court records, company filings, sales data and independent histories.
- “Follow-Me-Home” is observation with purchasers, not the union-linked participatory design defined in Chapter 14; the profile conflates the terms.
- User studies emphasize middle/upper-middle-income households and may omit low-income, unbanked, disabled, multilingual and nontraditional households.
- Financial software also shapes privacy, data security, financial categorization and household power, absent from the success narrative.

## Chapter 14: Design for People at Work `[543076, 577319)`

- Sarah Kuhn starts from nonchoice: most workers do not choose workplace software or control when/how it is used.
- Trouble Ticketing System: telephone repair automation suppresses consultation/training, fragments troubleshooting and penalizes unrecorded cooperative work.
- Big Bank: frequent “exception” freezes lead tellers/manager to share an officer card, undermining formal security while restoring judgment and throughput.
- HELP system: worker assistance signaling is useful; machine-status/individual-time monitoring misrepresents setup and skilled feed-rate adjustment. Union agreement blocks formal disciplinary use but not informal pressure.
- Systems make organizational representations of work; measured categories gain false objectivity and tacit coordination disappears.
- Workarounds are evidence about misfit, not merely user error or resistance.
- Costs listed: displacement/insecurity, intensification, monitoring, redefinition of skill, autonomy loss, stress, repetitive-strain and cardiovascular risk.
- “163 additional annual hours,” 1984 9to5 stress survey, labor-market and health associations must be checked in original studies and updated research.
- Human-centered manufacturing design places skill, judgment and learning alongside technical considerations.
- Participatory design requires access to relevant information, independent positions and decision participation. Scandinavian collective-resource work explicitly supports labor/industrial democracy and unions.
- UTOPIA with Nordic Graphic Workers’ Union uses joint visits, mockups, work-organization games and craft/tool ideals, not interviews alone.
- Easy cases correct accidental misunderstanding; hard cases concern deliberate strategies based on low wage, low skill, replaceability, pace and limited rest.
- RSI distinction: force/posture may be design accidents; repetition/no recovery may be business design goals.
- Kuhn proposes worker rights, unions/codetermination and professional ethics as supports when management goals conflict with autonomy.
- Strong evidence value: the chapter directly supplies the critical labor/power boundary missing from many earlier customer-centered chapters.
- It remains a 1996 US/Scandinavian synthesis and needs worker/union originals, project archives, racial/gender/disability analysis and platform-era updates.

## Profile 14: Participatory Design `[577843, 586639)`

- Historical chain: early-1970s Norway, Kristen Nygaard and Iron and Metalworkers Union; national codetermination agreement; Swedish DEMOS in late 1970s; UTOPIA with Swedish/Danish researchers and Nordic Graphic Workers’ Union.
- DEMOS locomotive-repair collaboration moves from disputed time standards to management prerogative, deskilling, production planning, materials and bargaining principles.
- UTOPIA “tool perspective” joins skilled practical understanding with technical possibilities; role-play, low-fidelity mockups and design-by-doing support mutual learning.
- TIPS reportedly tested at newspapers and sold to an image-processing firm; implementation, adoption, union evaluation and outcome need original project records.
- Four Greenbaum/Kyng points: evolved work practice; human actors not factors; situated tasks; work as social cooperation.
- Erickson’s four participation dimensions: directness, duration, scope and decision control. This is useful for distinguishing consultation from power sharing.
- The profile says industry was absorbing some techniques, but participation can be reduced when decision control is absent.

## Reflection `[587145, 599845)`

- Editors leave integration to readers and return to professionalization, education and quality.
- Software design is presented as cross-disciplinary, requiring representation, communication and enough understanding to coordinate programmers, graphic designers, database architects, marketers and users.
- Profession does not mean cookbook: formal methods/guidelines can make “the good better and the bad difficult,” while judgment/critique remain.
- Education options—university, institute, workplace; computer science, interdisciplinary program; course, studio, apprenticeship—are kept open.
- Quality combines objective/subjective, personal/political dimensions; professional peer critique, client feedback, awards and public discussion are proposed.
- 1996 ACM `interactions` design awards and planned 1997 Lund Qualiteque are prospective professional institutions. Later existence/outcomes cannot be inferred.
- Reliance on peer-recognized designers and awards can reproduce gatekeeping; quality must also include accessibility, safety, labor, public accountability and affected-user judgment.

## Whole-book synthesis and textbook boundary

- The volume documents a 1992–1996 attempt to define software design through professional manifestos, cross-disciplinary analogies, studio education, project profiles and workplace democracy.
- Its strongest conceptual chain is:
  1. software creates situated worlds/conceptual models;
  2. form, content, context and genre jointly shape meaning;
  3. design advances through iterative representations, prototypes and backtalk;
  4. organizations distribute authorship, goals, resources and constraints;
  5. workplace systems encode power and must include worker decision rights.
- Evidence types must stay separate:
  - participant interviews and professional manifestos;
  - consultancy/company success cases;
  - educational case studies;
  - secondary historical profiles;
  - labor/participatory-design research;
  - prospective forecasts and institution-building.
- The edited volume is US/UK/Scandinavian-centered and strongly connected to Stanford, MIT, PARC, Apple, IDEO, Intuit, DMI-like professional networks and ACM. Corporate and elite-school access shapes its canon.
- Women contributors are present, especially De Young and Kuhn, but most inventor/leader genealogies are male; race, colonialism, disability and global production labor remain thin.
- Use chapter 14/profile 14 to correct “user/customer” rhetoric elsewhere: observation and usability do not equal participation when users lack information, independent position and decision control.
- No evidence for Vkhutemas, Chinese official policy/industry organizations/institutional design-education history, MUJI, contemporary Chinese brands or generative-AI capability.
- 1996 WWW, adaptive-interface, multimedia and “future” claims are historical expectations only, not evidence about current platforms, large models or AI ethics.
- No maps, flags, emblems or state/party symbols should be selected from any related material.
