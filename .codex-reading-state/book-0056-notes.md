## Range 0–24,000

### Chapter 1 opening: hybrid proliferation

- Daily newspaper examples connect ozone depletion, chemical firms, states, treaties, development rights; AIDS laboratories, governments, patients and pharmaceutical firms; chips, embryos, forests, tracking devices, industrial waste/ecology, contraception and HDTV standards.
- These stories mix science, politics, economy, law, religion, technology and representation, while institutional headings keep nature, society and discourse separated.
- The central empirical intuition: fragile networks link distant domains, yet established disciplines cut them into pure segments.
- “Hybrid” must not be written as vague mixture; Latour means concrete associations among human and nonhuman actors, knowledge claims, institutions, technologies, law and discourse.

### Translation/network and collective

- Science and technology studies tries to “retie the Gordian knot” across exact knowledge and power/nature and culture.
- Network is presented as more supple than system, more historical than structure and more empirical than complexity; translation/network follows associations.
- Examples cited: MacKenzie on missile guidance, Callon on fuel cells, Hughes on Edison’s filament, Latour on Pasteur/microbes and laboratory brain peptides.
- These studies are not reducible to nature, social interests or texts: they trace arrangements simultaneously real, collective and discursive.
- Latour reserves `collective` for associations of humans and nonhumans; `society` names only one part produced by social-science division.

### Crisis of the critical stance

- Three critical repertoires: naturalization (emblem Wilson), socialization (Bourdieu) and deconstruction (Derrida). Each gains power by excluding the other two and cannot adequately analyze hybrids.
- Constructed facts are not thereby reducible to society; objects are real but cannot be placed wholly “outside”; deconstruction alone misses the practices of double construction.
- Ozone depletion is used to demonstrate an entity at once natural, narrated and collective.
- Anthropology can weave myths, techniques, politics, religion and material practices together for non-Western peoples but is institutionally prevented from treating “modern” Western collectives symmetrically.
- Latour argues that if anthropology of the modern world is possible, the definition of modernity must change.

## Critical cautions already visible

- “We/Western societies,” “moderns,” “premoderns,” “the tropics” and examples such as Arapesh, Achuar, Koreans and Chinese risk reproducing the Great Divide even while criticizing it; later chapters must be checked for the proposed correction.
- The Kurds comparison is a rhetorical analogy, not evidence about Kurdish history.
- AIDS, Africa, reproductive politics and environmental crises appear as examples in a philosophical argument; their historical facts, affected voices and current interpretation require specialist sources.
- Ozone, CFCs, epidemiology, corporate action and international treaties cannot be cited factually from this essay without primary scientific/policy sources.
- No design-history evidence yet; likely use is methodological/theoretical: hybrids, mediation, networks, nonhumans, modern constitution and political representation.

## Range 24,000–72,000

### 1989 and the double crisis

- Latour makes 1989 a conventional but useful symbol: collapse of state socialism and emergence of global environmental crisis jointly undermine the twin modern promises of social emancipation and mastery of nature.
- He criticizes both socialism’s exploitation/destruction and the North/West’s ability to protect some people and landscapes by exporting poverty and ecological damage.
- Modern, antimodern and postmodern responses all remain trapped by the same double framework; the proposed nonmodern position begins from the possibility that “we have never been modern.”
- This geopolitical compression is philosophical rhetoric, not a balanced history of 1989, socialism, capitalism or environmental governance. Europe-wide and global factual claims require specialist political/environmental histories and affected-region sources.

### Modernity as translation plus purification

- `Translation`/mediation creates mixtures or hybrids of nature and culture; `purification` creates distinct ontological zones of humans and nonhumans.
- Modern efficacy depends on keeping the practices conceptually separate: purification authorizes, and simultaneously obscures, accelerating hybrid production.
- Looking at both practices together makes retrospective “never modern” diagnosis possible and challenges the irreversible arrow that separates victorious moderns from archaic others.
- Latour’s provisional comparative hypothesis—that other cultures conceive hybrids and therefore restrict their proliferation—is explicitly admitted to be coarse and risks a new essentializing asymmetry.
- Political proposal preview: slow, redirect and regulate proliferating “monsters” by officially representing them; perhaps extend democracy to things.

### The Modern Constitution

- Modernity is not only humanism: it jointly produces humans, nonhumans and a “crossed-out God,” masks their joint birth and treats them separately while hybrids multiply below.
- Capital-C Constitution is an analytic metaphor for allocation of properties, rights, capacities and associations among humans/nonhumans, analogous to but broader than a political constitution.
- Comparative anthropology should describe Western scientific and political institutions together, without using modern metaphysics as the privileged measuring point.
- Latour turns to Shapin and Schaffer’s *Leviathan and the Air-Pump* as an exemplary, not exhaustive, account of the Boyle/Hobbes settlement.

### Boyle: laboratory, fabricated facts and witnessing

- Boyle’s costly, unreliable air pump is treated as early “Big Science”: glass, seals, pistons, lock chambers, technicians and controlled public witnessing make phenomena possible.
- Boyle replaces apodeictic certainty with credible gentlemen’s witnessed `matters of fact`; facts are fabricated under controlled conditions yet not thereby dismissed as arbitrary artifacts.
- Latour stresses invisible technicians, leaks, gaskets, cranks and scale changes. Laboratory realism is anchored “down there” on benches and practices, not in an abstract external reality.
- Local experimental facts travel only as instruments, skills, texts and communities are replicated. Universality is network extension and stabilization, not escape from practice.
- Historical caution: gentlemanly credibility excluded “Papists, atheists, sectaries,” ordinary divers and technicians; nonhuman testimony was embedded in classed, religious and gendered authority.

### Hobbes: unified representation and anti-transcendence

- Hobbes seeks civil peace through a unified sovereign representing the multitude; he rejects competing appeals to God, active matter or experimental factions.
- His materialism and biblical interpretation are political as well as philosophical: Knowledge and Power should remain unified, with no independent laboratory court of appeal.
- The conflict is not simply correct science versus obsolete politics; Boyle and Hobbes construct different distributions of scientific and political authority.
- Latour treats political representation (citizens/sovereign/social contract) and scientific representation (witnesses/nonhumans/matters of fact) as two halves of one settlement.

### Mediation and nonhuman testimony

- Shapin and Schaffer’s methodological achievement is to make disputes about God, King, matter and morality pass through the material practice of the air pump instead of treating “context” as an external macro-cause.
- Boyle adapts legal and scriptural witnessing to instruments and inert bodies: nonhumans are granted capacity to register effects before trustworthy human witnesses.
- Scientific articles combine old exegesis with inscriptions produced by instruments.
- Latour argues Shapin and Schaffer remain asymmetrical when they deconstruct scientific facts but accept the macro-social/political repertoire too readily; he seeks a position that generates both content and context.

### Design-history relevance

- Strong methodological bridge to laboratory/instrument design, standardization, black-boxing, infrastructure, distributed authorship and evidence production.
- Air pump case can support a chapter on design as sociotechnical mediation only via Shapin/Schaffer and primary archives; Latour is a secondary theoretical reading.
- Do not aestheticize suffocated animals or erase technicians and excluded witnesses. Animal experimentation, labor, class/religious exclusion and colonial circulation require independent histories.

## Range 72,000–120,000

### Scientific and political representation

- Latour rejects using an already-stable “macro-society,” power or interest to explain science; Hobbesian political categories must be historicized just as Boyle’s scientific categories are.
- Boyle and Hobbes create a division between representation of things through laboratories and representation of citizens through social contract. Both rely on authorized spokespersons who translate and can betray their constituents.
- Scientific facts are mute yet made to speak through instruments and disciplined representatives; citizens are many yet made to speak through sovereign representatives.
- This provides a useful but normative bridge to design representation: any claim to speak for users, materials, environments or publics requires examination of authorization, instruments, exclusions and possible betrayal.

### Four constitutional guarantees

1. Nature is transcendent—not made by humans, merely discovered.
2. Society is immanent—made freely by humans.
3. Nature and Society, plus purification and mediation, remain officially separated.
4. God is crossed out of nature and politics but retained as remote arbiter and private spirituality.

- In practice the guarantees reverse: laboratories fabricate/mobilize nature; durable society depends on objects and institutions that exceed individuals.
- Alternating transcendence and immanence makes modern critique unusually flexible: actors may claim necessity/natural law, freedom/social construction, social necessity or technical possibility as convenient.
- The Constitution renders mediation unrepresentable while allowing hybrids to proliferate at expanded scale.

### Critical power and “invincibility”

- First Enlightenment: natural causality criticizes superstition and illegitimate power.
- Second Enlightenment/social sciences: social laws and ideology critique challenge naturalization and scientism.
- Marxism is presented as combining both critical repertoires; this is Latour’s schematic philosophical reading, not a sufficient Marxist intellectual history.
- Modern critique can shift between nature and society so rapidly that objections are answered by changing the pole of explanation.
- Colonial expansion is acknowledged: modern collectives accused others of mixing humans/things while themselves remixing at unprecedented scale.

### Modernity as effective performative, not illusion

- Latour explicitly rejects a false-consciousness/debunking account. The Constitution is effective because actors genuinely perform both purification and mediation without considering them together.
- Analogy with revisionist history of the French Revolution: “revolution” and “modernity” are modalities that organize action and historiography, not transparent descriptions of processes.
- Scale change results from amplifying human/nonhuman contacts, not actually separating them.

### Premodern/modern comparison and serious problem

- Latour claims premodern nature-cultures conceptually saturate hybrids and therefore limit their proliferation, while moderns ignore hybrid consequences and multiply them.
- He cites Lévi-Strauss, Descola, Clastres and Horton, using “native,” “savage thought,” “cold societies” and a modern/premodern contrast.
- Even if the target is Western superiority, this argument essentializes many societies, relies on older anthropology, implies technological constraint through conceptual closure and risks reinstating the Great Divide.
- Textbook use must foreground this contradiction and prioritize Indigenous/local scholars, colonial histories and empirical environmental/technology studies. Do not reproduce “savage/premodern” labels as neutral categories.

### End of denunciation opening

- If the Constitution can no longer provide independent courts of appeal in Nature, Society or God, traditional critical denunciation loses secure high ground.
- Latour seeks a critique that reveals constitutional organization without repeating a modern debunking stance; later solution must be assessed for whether it actually supplies accountability and justice.

## Range 120,000–168,000

### Sociology of criticism and practical judgement

- Boltanski/Thévenot shift criticism from privileged method to topic: compare actors’ grammars and principles of justice instead of assuming analysts alone expose hidden motives.
- Latour opposes rigid denunciation to case-sensitive arrangement, negotiation, compromise and triage; objects participate in tests of judgement.
- This can discipline textbook voice—distinguish historical actors’ accusations, standards and compromises from authorial verdicts—but must not dissolve material harm, unequal power or warranted accountability into neutral negotiation.
- Girard is criticized for making objects mere stakes of mimetic desire; Latour wants objects to count in moral arrangements.

### Nonmodern/amodern stance

- Nonmodern inquiry follows purification and hybrid production together; it “deploys/adds/sorts” rather than only unveils/subtracts/denounces.
- Postmodernism remains inside modern temporal succession while losing faith in its guarantees; antimodernism reverses values but accepts the same entities and irreversible time.
- `Never modern` is a retrospective re-reading, not a claim that institutions, industrialization or colonial change did not occur.
- Latour says networks reveal important but incremental extensions, circulations and changes rather than absolute ruptures. This is a historiographic intervention, not grounds for minimizing violence, dispossession or structural transformation.
- His “Middle Kingdom, as vast as China” is a metaphor and not evidence about China; do not use it in Chinese design-history narration.

### Crisis by proliferation

- Frozen embryos, expert systems, digital machines, sensor robots, hybrid corn, databases, psychotropic drugs, instrumented whales and gene synthesis no longer fit pure object/subject categories.
- Environmental and demographic examples expose entities simultaneously made by humans and beyond any individual’s control, local and global.
- The “third estate of things” and “Third World” analogy is rhetorically forceful but risks conflating political constituencies and object ontology; affected peoples cannot be reduced to a philosophical figure.

### Quasi-objects/quasi-subjects

- Social theory alternates between objects as empty projections of society and hard causes determining society; society likewise appears omnipotent or powerless.
- Science studies’ symmetrical treatment of hard science reveals the inadequacy of both social constructionism and naive scientific realism.
- Quasi-objects are more fabricated/social/collective than “hard nature” yet more real/nonhuman/objective than projection screens. They help build collectives rather than simply receiving social meaning.
- They occupy the locus dualism and dialectical feedback fail to conceptualize. This is relational positioning, not a claim that object and subject properties disappear.

### Philosophy survey and its limits

- Kant: inaccessible thing-in-itself and transcendental subject; mediations are intermediaries transmitting pure forms.
- Hegel/dialectics: turns separation into contradiction and historical motor, yet retains poles.
- Phenomenology: intentionality spans an unresolved tension; Bachelard intensifies scientific objectivity and autonomous imagination.
- Habermas: communicative subjects are protected from technical/object rationality; Latour attacks his lack of empirical network cases.
- Lyotard/postmodernism: technology becomes ahuman and discourse detached; Latour calls this hyper-incommensurability.
- This is an openly polemical, highly compressed canon of European philosophy. It cannot replace direct reading or specialist history and largely omits feminist, postcolonial, Black, Indigenous and non-European thought.

### Design-history use

- `Quasi-object` can guide analysis of designed artifacts that distribute relations, but should not become a label detached from documented networks.
- Digital-machine and expert-system references show an early-1990s horizon; they do not support current AI claims.
- Avoid using Latour’s “small extension” rhetoric to understate industrial labor, imperialism, environmental damage or institutional power.

## Range 168,000–216,000

### Semiotic turn and Heidegger

- Semiotics gives discourse autonomy and treats texts as mediators that produce meaning, references and subject positions; this is valuable against language-as-transparent-conduit models.
- Its limit is bracketing referents and social contexts, making it difficult to follow entities that are simultaneously real, discursive and collective.
- Latour’s Heidegger critique: Being is not absent from ordinary science, machines, economics or daily artifacts; networks are “full of Being.” Empirical inquiry should follow these mediations rather than reserve authenticity for Black Forest paths or handcrafted objects.
- This philosophy survey is polemical. Claims about Barthes, Eco, Heidegger and others must be attributed to Latour, not taught as settled interpretations.

### Modern temporality and the invention of rupture

- Calendar time differs from historicity/intensity; Latour uses Péguy’s *Clio* and Serres to argue that time is produced by connections and classifications among entities.
- Modern temporality aligns nature/culture purification with a past/future asymmetry: the past supposedly confuses humans/things, while the future purifies them through revolutions.
- “Progress” and “decadence” are paired outcomes of arranging quasi-objects into coherent cohorts and suppressing heterogeneous continuities.
- Scientific/technological entities appear as miraculous ruptures only when instruments, institutions, predecessors, labor and controversies are erased.
- Examples—Boyle, Lavoisier, vaccination, atomic bomb, computer—show why design and technology histories should reconstruct mediation rather than use “BC/after computer” epochal slogans.

### Polytemporality and triage

- Contemporary assemblages combine entities and practices of many ages; hammer/electric drill example illustrates polytemporality.
- Spiral rather than linear time: the past is revisited, protected, recombined and reinterpreted rather than simply surpassed.
- Stable “tradition” and radical “innovation” are symmetrical constructions; invented-tradition scholarship is cited.
- `Triage/sorting` produces temporal classifications. It can help analyze revival, reuse and maintenance, but must include who has authority to sort, whose heritage is protected, and what harm cannot be recombined away.
- Latour’s Bedouin/!Kung and “land of contrasts” examples challenge Western temporal privilege but still objectify named peoples; use local scholarship instead.

### Mediators versus intermediaries

- Intermediary is imagined as transmitting a pre-existing cause/meaning with possible loss; mediator transforms, translates and helps create both what moves and the entities connected.
- Modern explanation starts from pure Nature/Society poles, disassembles hybrids and reconstitutes them through intermediaries.
- Nonmodern explanation starts from mediating practices and treats purified Nature/Society as provisional outcomes.
- Purification itself becomes a documented practice requiring instruments, institutions and skill, not a self-evident conceptual distinction.
- This is among the most useful design-history principles: artifacts, standards, exhibitions, interfaces and documents should be tested for transformations and redistributed capacities, not labelled neutral carriers.

### Copernican counter-revolution

- Nature and Subject/Society become outcomes orbiting collectives of humans/nonhumans, rather than explanatory starting points.
- “Counter-revolution” is a philosophical inversion, not a denial of scientific reality or political subjects.
- The next sections must specify ontological variety, causation and evidence so the mediator concept does not become unlimited metaphor.

## Range 216,000–240,000

### History as transformation

- Starting from Nature/Society treats them as eternal explanatory urns; intermediaries merely reveal or materialize pre-existing forces.
- Starting from mediators shows the air pump, Boyle, English society, theology, scholarly sociability and Nature changing together.
- Actors translate, redefine, redeploy and may betray what they carry; history “does something,” and entities gain trajectories rather than fixed origins.
- This supports design history attentive to project change, appropriation, maintenance and stabilization; it does not mean any narrative relation is equally causal.

### Cause, thing and accusation

- Drawing on Serres, Latour connects legal `causa`/`res` and scientific cause/thing: facts and objects emerge through tests, courts, witnesses and substitutions.
- Boyle relocates accusation from persons/opinions to instrumented nonhuman indicators, producing a pacification claim around matters of fact.
- The etymological/philosophical generalization is suggestive but requires linguistic and legal-historical verification; it cannot itself establish all European language histories.
- Scientific objectivity should not be reduced to court metaphor. Instruments, repeatability, material resistance and institutional witnessing all need concrete case evidence.

### Variable ontologies and stabilization

- A second analytic dimension registers movement from unstable event to stabilized essence; natural/social “longitude” alone cannot locate an entity.
- The vacuum’s essence is not one fixed position but its trajectory through experimental artifact, contested phenomenon and stabilized reality.
- Nature/Society are “cooled” outcomes of mediation, analogous to tectonic plates; inquiry descends to sites where mixtures and classifications are made.
- Existence precedes essence is extended from humans to all actants. This is Latour’s philosophical claim; textbook use should phrase it as an analytic proposal.
- Stabilization is not truthlessness: a well-established entity can be real while still having a documented history of construction and circulation.

### Four repertoires reconnected

1. external/material reality;
2. social bonds, passions and institutions;
3. discourse, narrative and signification;
4. Being/historicity.

- Quasi-objects trace networks that can be real, collective, discursive and existential without being reduced to one repertoire or divided into percentages.
- For design history this supplies a fourfold evidence checklist: material performance; social/institutional association; representation and language; temporal/ontological stabilization.
- It remains a high-level theoretical schema. Strong causal or ethical claims still require affected people, archival records, material tests and comparative evidence.

## Range 240,000–288,000

### Symmetry and generalized symmetry

- Bloor’s symmetry principle demands the same kinds of causes for truth/error, knowledge/belief and successful/failed science, preventing winners from being self-explanatory.
- Latour argues Bloor remains asymmetrical by using Society as the stable cause of both; generalized symmetry instead follows the joint attribution of human/nonhuman properties without starting from external Nature or social power.
- Nature and Society are therefore objects of explanation, not ready-made explanatory hooks.
- Symmetry is a methodological starting discipline, not a finding that all claims are equally true or all outcomes equally just. It should reveal why discrepancies and asymmetries arise.

### Two Great Divides

- Internal divide: Nature versus Society/human versus nonhuman.
- External divide: “the West” with science versus other peoples with merely cultural representations.
- Latour argues the external hierarchy exports the internal Constitution; Western trade, conquest, telescopes, syringes and science are narrated as access to reality unavailable to others.
- Lévi-Strauss’s defense of “savage thought” is criticized for measuring it against information theory, biology and modern instruments, leaving Western science transcendent.
- The chapter explicitly connects the Great Divide to colonial ships, gunboats, white-man’s burden, imperialism and the categorization of women’s bodies, children, animals, madness and popular culture as mixture zones.
- Yet Latour’s own enumerations—China, Amerindians, Inuit, Tasmanian Aboriginal peoples, Azande, Barouya, etc.—often treat radically different peoples as examples. The critique therefore requires decolonial and local correction.

### Anthropology “at home”

- Anthropology should not study only Western margins, beliefs and risk perceptions while leaving central infrastructures, engineering, economics and science to other disciplines.
- A symmetric metro study would follow engineers, drivers, directors, riders, the employer-state, technology and operations together, not only graffiti.
- The same tools should examine laboratories and other collectives without imposing either Western dualism abroad or romantic monism at home.
- The goal is neither to scandalize everyone nor prove all actors wrong, but to compare how collectives actually distribute humans/nonhumans, signs/things and authority.

### “There are no cultures” and natural relativism

- Culture as a bounded representation is, for Latour, an artifact of bracketing off a singular Nature. Comparative units should be `natures-cultures`/collectives.
- Absolute relativism isolates incommensurable cultures; cultural relativism assigns different viewpoints on one unconstructed Nature; “particular universalism” secretly lets the West define that Nature.
- Natural relativism compares joint production of humans, divinities and nonhumans without denying material reality or treating science as local fiction.
- Title claim is theoretical and provocative. It must not erase languages, institutions, identities, colonial histories or communities’ own terms.

### Sizeable differences

- Symmetry first levels the scale, then asks how collectives become asymmetrical through scale, enlistment, network length and mobilization.
- Latour argues differences are “of size,” not a metaphysical Great Divide; modern sciences/technologies enroll many nonhumans and connect larger collectives.
- This is important for infrastructure and design-system history, but reducing domination to network size risks under-theorizing capital, law, coercion, race, colonialism, gender, military force and resistance.
- Maps are mentioned as mobilization devices, but per project rule map images will not be used.

## Range 288,000–336,000

### Archimedes and technical-political composition

- Plutarch’s Archimedes story joins compound pulleys, royal power and siege technology: mechanical proportion changes political/military force.
- A second move purifies mathematical theory from “vulgar” engineering and necessity, erasing the alliance that produced new power.
- Latour’s formula—science as politics pursued by other means that work because treated as radically other—should be attributed, not converted into “science is only politics.”
- Plutarch is an ancient literary source, not direct project documentation. Syracuse, military engineering and Archimedes require ancient-history/source criticism.

### Relationism and metrology

- Absolute universalism and absolute relativism both imagine an already-given measuring standard.
- `Relativist relativism`/relationism studies how commensurability is built through instruments, metrological chains, dictionaries, standards, museums, expeditions, files and negotiations.
- Universal effects are network achievements; measures are also “measuring measures” that construct relations.
- Anthropological catalogues and expeditions participated in colonial measurement and domination. This needs more than Latour’s institution list, including provenance, coercion, classification and return/restitution histories.

### Disenchantment critique

- Latour denies that science/technology automatically produce a cold, fully rationalized, soulless world. Products such as abstraction, universality and organization do not arise from abstract/universal/organized processes.
- He distinguishes nouns (real effects: rationality, science, system, organization) from explanatory adjectives/adverbs that merely repeat the outcome.
- This is methodologically valuable: explain standardization, bureaucracy and technological efficacy by concrete work rather than naming them as causes.
- However, critique of totalization must not trivialize workers’ alienation, racial capitalism, bureaucratic violence, disability exclusion or ecological harm. The existence of seams does not make harms unreal.

### Long networks remain local at all points

- Rail, telephone, sewer, broadcast and scientific/metrological networks extend through paid branchings, stations, instruments, subscriptions, decoders, labor and maintenance.
- They are connected lines rather than universally filled surfaces; reach, access and failure must be documented at each connection.
- Scientific facts are compared to frozen fish requiring an unbroken cold chain: circulation depends on instruments, schools, laboratories, standards and interpretation.
- Early-1990s bytes/bauds/databank/CNN language is historical and cannot describe current digital platforms without updates.
- Project rule: do not use map images even though mapping/metrology is discussed.

### Leviathan as skein of networks

- Large organizations—IBM, Red Army, French Education Ministry, markets—are not separate macro-substances; their scale is achieved through documents, commands, offices, calculations, flowcharts, invoices and localized interactions.
- Local/global are viewpoints on networks, not intrinsic types. The key research object is the intermediary arrangement that connects them.
- This supports design histories of standards, corporate identity, public service and infrastructure: trace forms, manuals, procurement, labor and maintenance instead of assuming a seamless “system.”
- Latour’s dismissal of macro-totality should be balanced with structural history. Laws, capital, empire and state coercion can have patterned effects even when materially enacted through networks.

### Margins and the “ordinary” scale of crimes

- Latour critiques modern/antimodern agreement that a total technical center opposes authentic margins, spirit, warmth and locality.
- He urges measuring imperialism, capitalism and domination without granting them supernatural totality, because totalization may obscure points of intervention.
- His statement that crimes are “ordinary” is ethically dangerous if detached from context. Genocide, slavery, colonial dispossession, war and environmental destruction must be named through victims’/affected communities’ evidence and cannot be normalized by network vocabulary.
- Useful retained rule: do not infer total mechanism from large effects; but equally do not infer insignificance from distributed causation.

## Range 336,000–360,000

### Transcendence as delegation

- Latour rejects Nature, Society, Discourse, God or Being as one homogeneous container for all collectives.
- Networks are not immersed in a pre-given medium. Durability and presence are achieved through hazardous passages, delegates, mediators, messengers, instruments and representatives.
- `Transcendence without a contrary`/delegation means something remains present across a pass; solidity must be paid for through mediation.
- This can guide analysis of representation, standards and institutional continuity, but the spiritual/angelic vocabulary is philosophical rhetoric, not an empirical ontology to state as fact.

### Impossible modernization

- Modernization is described as a violent project of assigning hybrids to Nature or Society while destroying local natures-cultures and presenting purification as future civilization.
- Latour explicitly acknowledges force, bloodshed and destruction but compresses colonial and developmental histories. These need region-specific evidence and affected voices.
- Postmodern loss of faith adds “hyperreality” after material disruption without diagnosing how hybrid proliferation contradicted constitutional purification.
- Nonmodern diagnosis neither continues modernization nor simply opposes it; it seeks to make mediation public.

### Final examination: proposed selective inheritance

From modern practices, Latour wants to retain:

- hybrid production, long networks, traces, delegates, relative universals;
- research, invention, tinkering, scale and stabilized external objects;
- legal/political subjects and differentiated Nature.

He rejects:

- claims of radical Western difference, pure rationality/materialism/spirituality;
- irreversible rupture, Nature/culture dichotomy, fact/value and thing/sign absolutes;
- privileged denunciation.

From so-called premoderns, he wants to retain:

- explicit attention to hybrids, multiple nonhumans and multiple temporalities;
- abundant transcendences and refusal of durable pure poles.

He rejects:

- territorial scale limits, scapegoating, ethnocentrism and lasting non-differentiation.

### Major critical issue

- This sorting claims authority to select “best” traits from vast, heterogeneous peoples and Western institutions. It risks a new universal taxonomy and repeats the very abstraction criticized earlier.
- “Premodern limits” are asserted without adequate empirical evidence; territoriality, scapegoating and nondifferentiation cannot be generalized.
- A textbook should retain the methodological challenge—trace purification/mediation together—while refusing civilizational scoring.

## Range 360,000–415,457

### Humanism redistributed

- Latour refuses a human essence isolated from objects, animals, machines, society, religion or exchange.
- `Morphism` names the intersection of techno-, zoo-, physio-, ideo-, theo-, socio- and psychomorphisms; the anthropos is a “weaver of morphisms.”
- Human agency is delegated, mediated, distributed and materially supported; machines contain human arrangements and humans are constituted through artifacts.
- This can correct designer-genius and human-versus-technology narratives, but equal analytic attention to nonhumans must not erase human rights, responsibility, consent or unequal vulnerability.
- Latour’s confident claims that machines cannot threaten humans need correction: socio-technical systems can injure, surveil, displace or discriminate even if human arrangements inhabit them.

### Four guarantees of the proposed nonmodern Constitution

1. **Nonseparability**: quasi-objects/quasi-subjects and mediation must be publicly represented; institutions that block continuous tracing are harmful.
2. **Objectivization and subjectivization as joint outcomes**: objective Nature and free political subjects remain valuable stabilized consequences, not opposed starting causes.
3. **Free recombination/polytemporality**: no automatic choice between archaic/modern, local/global, cultural/universal, natural/social; actors can sort associations.
4. **Regulated hybrid production**: replace clandestine proliferation with deliberate, commonly agreed, slower and publicly accountable production.

- Purification is retained as a special, documented outcome of mediation rather than banned.
- The fourth guarantee is the democratic/ethical core but remains under-institutionalized: who participates, how nonhumans are represented, how conflicts are decided and how unequal power is corrected are not specified.

### Parliament of Things

- Scientific representation of nonhumans and political representation of citizens become one problem of authorized spokespersons, translation and betrayal.
- The ozone case gathers scientific, industrial, labor, electoral, state, meteorological, legal and material representatives around the same quasi-object.
- There are neither naked truths nor naked citizens; representatives and mediators must be examined together.
- Parliament is a constitutional metaphor/proposal, not a documented institution or proven governance model.
- A design-history application should reconstruct stakeholder/nonhuman representation, evidence devices, procurement, labor, public hearings, maintenance and redress; it should not pretend every entity literally votes.
- The concluding return to 1989 frames socialism/naturalism as joint failure. This remains a schematic Western philosophical narrative requiring independent political and environmental history.

## Whole-book synthesis

### Argument chain

1. Contemporary controversies proliferate hybrids that disciplinary Nature/Society/Discourse divisions cannot hold.
2. Modernity is an operational Constitution joining purification with hidden mediation, not a successfully achieved separation.
3. Boyle/Hobbes establish distinct scientific and political representations while laboratories, states, objects and citizens are co-produced.
4. Quasi-objects, mediators and variable stabilization replace explanations that start from fixed Nature or Society.
5. Modern linear time, radical revolution and Western Great Divide are effects of purification and arranged histories.
6. Generalized symmetry compares collectives without giving the West exclusive access to Nature, then studies real asymmetries through network scale and metrology.
7. A nonmodern Constitution would publicly represent and democratically regulate hybrid production in a Parliament of Things.

### Evidence types

- **P1 theoretical synthesis**: the book’s main status.
- **P1/P2 secondary historical reading**: Boyle/Hobbes is explicitly dependent on Shapin/Schaffer; Archimedes on Plutarch/Serres.
- **Illustrative current events**: ozone, AIDS, environment, technology, 1989; not researched case chapters.
- **Polemical intellectual history**: Kant, Hegel, phenomenology, Habermas, Lyotard, Heidegger, semiotics.
- **Anthropological comparisons**: dependent on older Western ethnographies and used schematically.
- **Normative proposal**: nonmodern Constitution and Parliament of Things; no institutional evaluation.

### Main value for *Modern and Contemporary Design History*

- Provides a historiographic method for artifacts as mediators, not passive forms/intermediaries.
- Connects design, laboratory apparatus, standards, infrastructure, documents, representation, maintenance and scale.
- Challenges rupture/progress narratives and supports polytemporal, trajectory-based histories.
- Requires simultaneous study of purification claims and hybrid production.
- Adds nonhuman/material evidence to public representation and design ethics.
- Corrects macro claims by tracing networks, but must be combined with political economy, labor, feminist, decolonial, disability and environmental histories.

### Major limitations/risks

- Speculative essay, deliberately sparse in empirical case material.
- Europe/Western philosophy and STS center; non-Western peoples filtered through older anthropology.
- Repeated modern/premodern, “savage,” “tropics,” “Third World” and civilizational language.
- Network-size explanation under-specifies law, capital, coercion, empire, race, gender and structural violence.
- Anti-denunciation/anti-totalization can weaken justified criticism if used without harm and power analysis.
- Human/nonhuman symmetry can obscure distinct moral/legal responsibility and human vulnerability.
- 1991–1993 technological horizon cannot support platform, climate-governance or AI claims today.
